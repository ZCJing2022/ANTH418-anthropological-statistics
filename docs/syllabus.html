Throughout this course, we will gather at UBC Vancouver’s Point Grey Campus, located on the traditional, ancestral, and unceded territory of the xʷməθkʷəy̓əm (Musqueam) people. This land has been a place of learning for the Musqueam for millennia.

Course Description

This course introduces statistical thinking and quantitative analysis for archaeology and anthropology. It is designed for students with little or no prior experience in statistics or programming. Its central goal is practical: students will learn how to move from an anthropological research question to an appropriate dataset, write understandable R code, conduct an appropriate statistical analysis, visualize the data and statistical results, and make a defensible anthropological interpretation.

The course combines fundamental concepts in research design, measurement, sampling, data quality, descriptive statistics, uncertainty, statistical inference, group comparison, association, and regression with sustained hands-on work in R, RStudio, and Quarto.

Each topic will be learned through a recurring workflow:

inspect and prepare the data → describe → visualize → analyze → visualize the result → interpret

Weekly assignments use real datasets drawn from recent published research in archaeology, biological anthropology, cultural anthropology, and linguistic anthropology. The principal graded case studies were selected using four criteria:

  1. the research question is anthropologically substantive;
  2. the associated publication dates after 2000;
  3. the underlying dataset is available online through a publication supplement or open research repository; and
  4. the statistical question can be addressed transparently with methods appropriate to an introductory course.

The emphasis is not on memorizing formulas or treating software output as an answer. Students will repeatedly be asked to understand how the data were produced, write and explain their own code, choose suitable statistical procedures, produce clear figures and tables, evaluate effect size and uncertainty, and connect statistical results to archaeological or anthropological questions while recognizing assumptions and limitations.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Demonstrate basic data, statistical, and digital literacy, including an understanding of variables, measurement scales, distributions, sampling, uncertainty, and common sources of bias.
  2. Translate an archaeological or anthropological research question into an explicit unit of analysis, set of variables, and appropriate statistical strategy.
  3. Write and explain introductory R code to import, inspect, clean, transform, summarize, sample, visualize, and analyze anthropological data.
  4. Use RStudio and Quarto to organize a reproducible workflow that integrates prose, code, tables, figures, and statistical output.
  5. Apply and interpret introductory statistical methods, including descriptive statistics, confidence intervals, permutation tests, t-tests, chi-square tests, ANOVA, correlation, and simple linear regression.
  6. Use ggplot2 and related R tools to explore distributions and relationships and to visualize statistical results, effect sizes, uncertainty, and model fit where appropriate.
  7. Evaluate data provenance, missingness, sampling structure, assumptions, and dependence among observations, and communicate effect size, uncertainty, and limitations rather than relying on statistical significance alone.
  8. Distinguish between statistical result, anthropological interpretation, and causal explanation, and evaluate quantitative claims in published archaeological and anthropological research.
  9. Use computational and generative-AI tools responsibly as aids to learning while retaining responsibility for code comprehension, statistical reasoning, verification, interpretation, and authorship.

Prerequisite

ANTH 100 (Introduction to Cultural Anthropology), ARCL 103 (Introduction to Archaeology), ARCL 140 (Bones: The Origins of Humanity), another Anthropology/Archaeology course, or permission of the instructor.

No previous statistics or programming course is required.

Core Readings and Learning Resources

Students are not expected to read any textbook from cover to cover. Each teaching week will normally include at least one focused reading explaining the statistical subject or R technique, and at least one reading connected directly to the week’s archaeological or anthropological case study.

Method readings should be used actively: students are encouraged to run the R examples while reading rather than treating the books as passive background material.

Case-Study Readings

Readings associated with the weekly datasets are listed in the course schedule. These readings are part of the statistical training. For each case, students should be able to identify:

  • the research question;
  • the unit of analysis;
  • the key variables and their measurement scales;
  • how the data were produced;
  • the analytical strategy;
  • the principal statistical result; and
  • what the evidence does and does not justify anthropologically.

Software and Computational Workflow

Students will use R, RStudio Desktop, and Quarto, all of which are free. Course examples will use a deliberately small and stable set of packages, especially tidyverse/ggplot2 and packages required for specific teaching datasets or analyses.

Most practical work will follow the same six-step workflow:

  1. Code: load the dataset, inspect its structure, select variables, and prepare the data in R.
  2. Describe: compute relevant descriptive statistics before running an inferential procedure.
  3. Visualize the data: use an appropriate plot to examine distributions, groups, outliers, or relationships.
  4. Analyze: run the statistical procedure in R and extract the quantities needed for interpretation.
  5. Visualize the result: where useful, graph group differences, uncertainty, fitted relationships, residuals, or other model results rather than presenting raw console output alone.
  6. Interpret: explain the statistical result in relation to archaeological or anthropological research questions, including assumptions and limitations.

All graded computational work should be completed in a Quarto (.qmd) document unless an assignment specifies otherwise. A submission should contain only the R output necessary to support the analysis; long unedited console dumps should be avoided.

Students should be able to re-render their document from a clean R session and explain every substantive line of code included in the submission.

Course Data and Reproducibility

All ten graded assignments use real data derived from recent published archaeological or anthropological research. The original data are publicly available online through a journal supplement, Zenodo, GitHub, or another research repository.

For teaching purposes, the instructor may distribute a frozen, documented teaching file derived from the published source dataset. Such files may:

  • retain only variables needed for the assignment;
  • standardize column names or file format;
  • preserve source row identifiers;
  • remove blank spreadsheet rows or source-identified invalid observations; and
  • restrict the analysis to a clearly documented subset when the full dataset is unnecessarily large or complex.

Teaching files will not contain fabricated observations. Each assignment dataset will be accompanied by a short provenance note identifying the publication, original data source, variables retained, and any transformations or exclusions made before distribution.

Some original publications use advanced methods such as Bayesian modeling, PCA, machine learning, mixed-effects models, or multivariate procedures that lie beyond ANTH418. Students are not expected to reproduce those methods. Instead, assignments use a clearly stated ANTH418-level question that can be answered with techniques taught in the course while preserving the original observations and archaeological or anthropological context.

Course Format, Assignments, and Due Dates

Class meetings combine concise explanation with substantial guided practice. In most two-class weeks, one class introduces the statistical logic and demonstrates the R workflow; the other functions primarily as a code-along/data-analysis lab in which students work with the week’s case-study dataset, create visualizations, conduct the analysis, and discuss interpretation. Weeks affected by holidays or the midterm break will be adjusted accordingly.

Weeks 2 and 3 emphasize software, data structure, research design, reproducibility, and practice with real research datasets but do not carry graded weekly assignments. The ten graded assignments are distributed in Weeks 4–9 and Weeks 11–14.

Students are expected to attend and participate regularly because debugging, interpretation, and statistical judgment are learned through practice. Attendance and participation account for 20% of the final grade. Absences covered by an approved academic concession, accommodation, or other recognized circumstance will be handled in accordance with UBC policy. Students who anticipate or experience an extended absence should contact the instructor as early as possible.

Weekly Assignments — 50%

There are ten graded assignments, each worth 5%, for a total of 50% of the final grade.

Each assignment asks students to submit four connected products:

  1. working and understandable R code;
  2. an appropriate statistical analysis;
  3. one or more purposeful visualizations; and
  4. a concise written interpretation tied to the anthropological research question.

Students may discuss code and statistical ideas with classmates during class unless instructed otherwise, but each student must understand the analysis and submit an independently written report.

Assignments are normally due one week after they are distributed. Exact dates and any necessary adjustments will be posted on Canvas.

Research Project — 30%

The research project asks students to carry an analysis from a published archaeological or anthropological study and its original data through a transparent and reproducible workflow. Unlike the weekly assignments, where datasets are curated for teaching, the project requires students to make and document more of their own analytical decisions while remaining within statistical methods appropriate to an introductory course.

The project will include the following steps:

  1. Select a published case study from a list provided by the instructor, or propose another case with an accessible original dataset subject to approval.
  2. Define one or more research questions that can be evaluated with the available data and methods learned in this course.
  3. Obtain and document the dataset, identify the unit of analysis, create or revise a codebook, and prepare the data for analysis.
  4. Write a reproducible R workflow that carries out the necessary data preparation and statistical analysis.
  5. Produce clear visualizations of the data and, where appropriate, statistical estimates, uncertainty, or model results.
  6. Evaluate assumptions, uncertainty, effect size, data quality, dependence, and other limitations.
  7. Compare the statistical findings with the interpretation in the published study where appropriate. Students are not expected to reproduce advanced methods that lie outside the course; instead, they should clearly distinguish their ANTH418-level analysis from the fuller analysis in the publication.
  8. Produce a clear Quarto report that integrates prose, code, tables, figures, results, and references.

Suitable public or course-supported data sources may include p3k14c, XRONOS, D-PLACE, Seshat, Kinbank, Cliopatria, the Comparative Archaeology Database when its relevant datasets are available, and the public repositories or journal supplements associated with the weekly case studies.

Students must verify that the chosen data are appropriate to their research question and that any dependence, repeated observations, spatial structure, or temporal structure is acknowledged.

Project outline: A 100–200 word outline identifying the case study, dataset, and proposed research question(s) is due Monday, 9 November 2026.

Final report: Approximately 10–12 pages, excluding references and data/code appendices, following the citation style of either American Anthropologist or American Antiquity. The report must be rendered to PDF from Quarto and should include enough executable R code and documentation for the analysis to be reproduced.

Submit the PDF together with the .qmd file, dataset(s), codebook/README, and any other files necessary to reproduce the analysis in a single ZIP file via Canvas by Friday, 11 December 2026.

The project will be evaluated on:

  • data provenance and documentation;
  • clarity and reproducibility of the R code;
  • appropriateness and accuracy of the statistical analysis;
  • quality of data and result visualizations;
  • interpretation of effect size and uncertainty;
  • treatment of assumptions and limitations;
  • quality of archaeological or anthropological interpretation;
  • clarity and organization; and
  • proper citation and attribution.

Policy on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), including large language models and systems that generate text, code, images, audio, or other content, is now part of the intellectual environment in which quantitative research and learning take place. This course therefore aims to teach students not simply whether AI can be used, but when it contributes to learning and when it interferes with it.

Guiding principle: augmentation rather than automation.

GenAI can be useful when it helps you ask a better question, clarify a statistical concept, understand an error message, debug code you have attempted, compare alternative analytical choices, test an interpretation, or identify weaknesses in reasoning or writing. It becomes counterproductive when it performs the statistical, programming, interpretive, or writing work that you are expected to learn to do yourself.

Permitted Use by Activity

Weekly assignments: GenAI may be used to clarify concepts, explain error messages, help debug code after you have attempted the task, suggest alternative plots or analysis strategies, or provide feedback on writing you have already produced. It may not replace the assigned analysis, produce an entire solution for submission, or substitute for your own interpretation of statistical output.

Research project: GenAI may assist with brainstorming, refining a research question, debugging, exploring possible analytical approaches, organizing a workflow, or providing feedback on a draft. It may not substitute for locating and evaluating scholarly sources, documenting or cleaning the data, creating the codebook, selecting and justifying methods, interpreting results, verifying references, or authoring the report.

In-class exercises or designated activities: Some activities may be AI-free when the purpose is to practice coding, interpretation, or statistical reasoning independently. Any such restriction will be stated by the instructor.

Students remain responsible for the accuracy, integrity, reproducibility, and authorship of everything they submit. You must be able to explain any R code, statistical procedure, output, figure, interpretation, or written argument included in your work.

Accuracy, Verification, and Disclosure

GenAI can produce plausible but incorrect statistical explanations, faulty R code, invented functions or packages, fabricated references, and oversimplified interpretations. Important claims must therefore be checked against original scholarly sources, course materials, R documentation, the actual dataset, and the statistical output generated by your own analysis.

GenAI output should not be treated as anthropological evidence or scholarly authority. When AI-generated material is itself quoted, reproduced, or analyzed as an object of study, its source should be identified in accordance with UBC guidance. Otherwise, permitted GenAI use should normally be disclosed through a brief AI Use Statement rather than cited as scholarship.

When GenAI materially contributes to submitted work in a context where its use is permitted, include a short statement at the end of the assignment or project identifying the tool, what it was used for, and how its suggestions were checked or revised. Full chat transcripts are not normally required, but students should retain relevant prompts and drafts in case clarification of the work process is needed.

Example AI Use Statement:
“I used [tool] to help interpret two R error messages and to suggest alternative ways to code a plot. I checked the suggested code against the course dataset and R documentation, revised it, and independently interpreted the final output.”

Undisclosed or unauthorized use of GenAI that substitutes for work a student is expected to perform may constitute academic misconduct.

Privacy, Access, and Instructor Use

Do not upload confidential information, restricted archaeological or anthropological data, culturally sensitive material, unpublished work belonging to others, student work, or any other information you are not authorized to share with a commercial GenAI system. Exercise similar caution with copyrighted books, articles, lecture slides, datasets, or other course materials.

No student is required to purchase a paid AI subscription for this course. All required coursework can be completed using course resources, R/RStudio, documentation, readings, office hours, and other permitted forms of assistance.

The instructor may use GenAI as a support tool in preparing teaching materials or routine course administration while remaining responsible for the accuracy, appropriateness, and academic judgment of all course content. Student work will not be submitted to external GenAI systems for assessment or analysis unless the system and use have been approved under applicable UBC privacy requirements. Final grading and substantive evaluation remain the responsibility of the instructor.

AI-detection software will not, by itself, be treated as sufficient evidence of academic misconduct. Questions about authorship or AI use may instead be addressed by discussing a student’s sources, reasoning, code, drafts, prompts where relevant, and analytical process.

If you are uncertain whether a particular use of GenAI is permitted, ask the instructor before submitting the work.

Please read UBC’s guidelines of Learning With GenAI.

Academic Concession and Accommodation for Students

Requests for extensions or other academic concessions will be considered in accordance with UBC and Faculty of Arts academic-concession policies. Students should contact the instructor as early as reasonably possible when circumstances affect their ability to complete coursework by a stated deadline.

If you miss marked coursework and are an Arts student, review the Faculty of Arts’ academic concession page and then complete Arts Academic Advising’s online academic concession form, so that an advisor can evaluate your concession case.

If you are a student in a different Faculty, please consult your Faculty’s webpage on academic concession, and then contact the instructor where appropriate.

Academic accommodations reduce barriers in the learning environment for students with disabilities or ongoing medical conditions. Students requiring academic accommodations must register with the Centre for Accessibility, which determines students’ eligibility in accordance with Policy LR7: Disability Accommodation Policy. Instructors are responsible for implementing approved accommodations in consultation with the Centre where necessary.

Students are not required to disclose the nature of a disability or ongoing medical condition to the instructor, and instructors should not request disability documentation. Students requiring accommodations should register with the Centre for Accessibility and provide their accommodation letter as early as possible.

Academic Integrity

Academic integrity is essential to the work of this course and to participation in the UBC academic community. Students are responsible for submitting work that represents their own learning and for appropriately acknowledging sources and permitted assistance.

Unauthorized collaboration, plagiarism, cheating, falsification, or unauthorized use of generative AI may constitute academic misconduct. If you receive assistance from a tutor or another person, you remain responsible for ensuring that the assistance stays within the limits permitted for the assignment.

If you are uncertain about what is permitted for an assignment, consult the instructor before submitting your work.

Please consult the UBC Academic Calendar’s Academic Misconduct regulations for the University’s expectations and procedures.

Suggestions

Students are strongly encouraged to make suggestions about the pace, examples, readings, assignments, or other aspects of the course. Feedback is especially welcome when a statistical concept or R workflow would benefit from another explanation or more practice.
