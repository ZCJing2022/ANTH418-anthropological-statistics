How to use this workbook

Week 4 used numerical summaries to describe center and spread. Week 5 adds visualization as statistical reasoning.

inspect → describe → visualize → compare → question → revise → investigate

EDA is not a list of chart types. It is an iterative habit of asking whether values, distributions, comparisons, and observations make sense.

Learning objectives

By the end, you should be able to explain EDA as inquiry; distinguish description from inference; choose graphics from variable structure; use the ggplot2 grammar; distinguish mapping from setting; investigate binning; combine summaries with raw observations; design group comparisons and facets; diagnose misleading displays; audit sample structure; explore Kinbank responsibly; and recognize phylogenetic and spatial non-independence.

Part I — EDA as disciplined visual reasoning

1. Description is not inference

Classify each statement as descriptive or inferential:

  1. “In this teaching sample, kin_term_count is strongly right-skewed.”
  2. “All human languages have right-skewed kinship systems.”
  3. “The sampled languages are unevenly distributed across macroareas.”
  4. “Macroarea causes differences in kinship terminology.”

Explain what additional design, uncertainty, or modelling would be needed before moving from description to inference.

Part II — Choose the graphic from variable structure

2. Different variables pose different visual tasks

Variable structure Typical task Useful graphics
Categorical counts, proportions, composition bar chart, grouped bars, mosaic plot
Numerical location, spread, shape, outliers histogram, boxplot
Two numerical variables relationship, clusters, outliers scatterplot

Choose a first graphic for languages by macroarea; distribution of kin_term_count; kin_term_count across macroareas; longitude versus latitude; and latitude versus kin_term_count. Explain the visual task.

Part III — The grammar of ggplot2

3. A graph is a specification

library(tidyverse)

toy <- tibble(
  group = rep(c("A", "B"), each = 5),
  x = 1:10,
  y = c(3, 5, 4, 6, 7, 5, 7, 8, 8, 10)
)

ggplot(toy, aes(x = x, y = y)) +
  geom_point()

The beginner grammar is data + aesthetic mappings + geom. The fuller grammar adds layers, statistical transformations, scales, coordinates, facets, and themes.

4. Mapping versus setting

ggplot(toy, aes(x = x, y = y, shape = group)) +
  geom_point(size = 3)
ggplot(toy, aes(x = x, y = y)) +
  geom_point(shape = 17, size = 3)

What is wrong with geom_point(aes(colour = "steelblue"))?

Variable inside aes(); fixed appearance outside aes().

Part VIII — Visual channels and misleading graphs

9. Match encoding to measurement scale

For magnitude/order, position and length are strong channels. For category identity, facets, hue, and a small number of shapes can work well. Why should colour carry information rather than decoration?

10. What can a graph hide?

Consider truncated axes, overplotting, unequal group sizes, proportions without denominators, too many colours, free facet scales, smoothers treated as truth, and outliers treated as errors. Explain how each could mislead interpretation.

Part XVI — Non-independence in cross-cultural data

19. Phylogenetic and spatial autocorrelation

Related languages may resemble one another because of common descent. Nearby languages may resemble one another because of contact, borrowing, regional history, or shared environments.

EDA can reveal dependence; it does not automatically correct for dependence.

Explain why twenty closely related languages are not necessarily twenty independent pieces of evidence; why proximity creates another dependence; what visualization can diagnose; and what it cannot fix.

Part XVII — Kinbank operationalization

20. A count is only one summary of a relational system

Explain the difference among a culturally specific kin word, a genealogical relation/type, a form-record count, and a structural distinction in a kinship system. Why is the choice of what to visualize also a theoretical choice?

Part XVIII — Integrated EDA challenge

21. Build a disciplined Kinbank exploration

  1. Identify the unit of analysis.
  2. Inspect variables and provenance.
  3. Check missingness.
  4. Visualize sample composition.
  5. Explore kin_term_count with more than one binning.
  6. Summarize its distribution numerically.

  13. End with one better question for later formal analysis.

Part XIX — Conceptual debugging

22. Diagnose the mistake

Identify the problem:

  1. “The graph is striking, therefore the population relationship is proven.”
  2. “I used aes(colour = "steelblue") to make all points blue.”
  3. “This histogram proves the distribution has exactly two modes.”
  4. “The boxplot is enough; I do not need to inspect observations.”
  5. “Free scales make every facet easier to compare quantitatively.”
  6. “There are 1,287 languages, therefore there are 1,287 independent cultural cases.”
  7. “kin_term_count is a direct measure of kinship complexity.”
  8. “Colouring points by family corrects phylogenetic autocorrelation.”
  9. “Missing macroarea is a cultural category.”
  10. “EDA should end with a conclusion rather than a better question.”
