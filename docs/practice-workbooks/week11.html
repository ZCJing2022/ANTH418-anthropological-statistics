Download Week 11 Practice Package (.zip)

How to use this workbook

Week 9 compared numerical outcomes between groups. Week 11 changes the statistical object: the evidence is categorical counts.

define categories → identify units → count → proportions → visualize → expected counts → observed vs. expected → residuals → effect size → archaeology

Week 11 principle: A chi-square test is one part of a larger argument. Define categories, compare observed counts with an explicit expectation, then return to archaeology.

Learning objectives

By the end, you should be able to distinguish categorical from numerical variables; identify the unit of analysis; distinguish counts from proportions; choose denominators correctly; distinguish goodness-of-fit from independence tests; construct contingency tables; explain expected counts; interpret chi-square; inspect expected counts and standardized residuals; calculate Cramer’s V; explain sparse-table alternatives; visualize categorical evidence; and interpret the Lesotho case without confusing association with causation.

Part III — Proportions have sampling uncertainty

3. Same proportion, different precision

\[ SE(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} \]

tibble(n = c(10, 100, 1000), p_hat = .50) |>
  mutate(
    se = sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n),
    lower_approx = p_hat - 1.96 * se,
    upper_approx = p_hat + 1.96 * se
  )

3  1000   0.5 0.0158        0.469        0.531

Part XXV — Return to Lesotho archaeology

25. Association is not archaeological explanation

The statistical result supports an association between archaeological layer and raw-material composition in the teaching dataset. Residuals show which cells contribute most strongly, and Cramer’s V describes the overall strength of association.

It does not identify a unique behavioral mechanism.

Possible explanations require archaeological argument involving procurement preference, mobility, source accessibility, exchange, technology, taphonomy, sampling, classification, and other contextual evidence.

The published Lesotho case also draws on independent evidence such as cortex form and technological ratios. The archaeological argument is therefore broader than a p-value.

Practice 25

Separate your interpretation into four layers:

  1. What was observed — raw-material counts and proportions across BAS, RBL-CLBRF, RF, and BARF.
  2. Statistical association — chi-square, expected counts, residuals, and Cramer’s V.
  3. Possible archaeological meaning — procurement, mobility, accessibility, exchange, technology, or formation processes.
  4. What cannot be established — a unique behavioral or environmental cause from the contingency table alone.

Part XXVI — Conceptual debugging

26. Diagnose the mistake

For each statement, identify the problem.

  1. “FineChert was coded 3, so its mean is larger than Dolerite coded 2.”
  2. “The layers have similar percentages, so sample sizes do not matter.”
  3. “Expected counts should be equal in every cell.”
  4. “One observed cell is below 5, so Pearson’s chi-square is automatically invalid.”
  5. “The p-value is tiny, so the association must be strong.”
  6. “The overall chi-square tells us exactly which cells differ.”
  7. “Cramer’s V tells us which cells drive the association.”
  8. “Because layer and raw material are associated, layer caused procurement choice.”
  9. “We should collapse categories until the test becomes significant.”
