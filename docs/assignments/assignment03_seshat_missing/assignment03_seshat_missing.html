ANTH418 Assignment 3: Missing Data in the Seshat Moralizing Gods Debate

NoteBefore you begin

Read the Week 6 Study Guide before starting this assignment.

The Study Guide explains the required concepts and R workflow for data transformation, missing values, the distinction between NA and a recorded zero, deletion and imputation as analytical decisions, and sensitivity analysis.

This assignment uses a deliberately small aggregated teaching dataset. The CSV contains three rows summarizing the outcome status of the 801 observations in the documented Seshat regression frame. It does not contain 801 individual rows.

Research question

How does treating unknown observations as known absences change the descriptive pattern of moralizing-gods outcomes in the documented Seshat regression frame?

Case Study

Seshat: Global History Databank was developed to organize historical and archaeological information about past societies in standardized form for large-scale comparative research (Turchin et al. 2015).

Whitehouse et al. (2019) used Seshat data to investigate the relationship between social complexity and moralizing gods. Their published analysis argued that moralizing gods generally appeared after large increases in social complexity.

Beheim et al. (2021) subsequently focused attention on the treatment of missing outcome data. In the documented 801-observation regression frame, they reported:

  • 299 observations in which moralizing gods were recorded as present;
  • 12 observations in which moralizing gods were recorded as absent; and
  • 490 observations in which the outcome was missing or unknown.

The critical issue is that the 490 unknown observations were treated in the disputed analysis as if they were known absences.

This creates a fundamental data distinction:

documented absence is not the same thing as missing information.

The ANTH418 teaching file preserves these three states explicitly. Your task is not to reproduce the original regression models. Instead, you will use descriptive summaries and visualization to examine how the apparent empirical pattern changes when the 490 unknown observations are represented in two different ways.

Assignment

Using data/seshat_missingness_counts.csv, conduct a descriptive sensitivity analysis of the missing-data decision at the center of the Seshat moralizing-gods debate.

You will:

  1. inspect the teaching dataset and confirm what one row represents;
  2. calculate counts and proportions for the three documented outcome statuses;
  3. visualize the data while preserving missing_unknown as its own category;
  4. construct an alternative summary in which missing_unknown is combined with known_absent;
  5. compare the two representations numerically and visually; and
  6. explain what the comparison shows about the consequences of treating unknown information as documented absence.

The assignment is about the effect of an analytical decision, not about deciding whether moralizing gods actually were present in the unknown cases.

4. Construct the alternative unknown → absent representation

Now reproduce the logic of the disputed coding decision at the aggregate level.

Do not overwrite dat. Create a new object.

recoded <- dat |>
  mutate(
    recoded_status = case_when(
      status == "known_present" ~ "present",
      status %in% c("known_absent", "missing_unknown") ~ "absent"
    )
  ) |>
  group_by(recoded_status) |>
  summarize(
    count = sum(count),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  mutate(
    proportion = count / sum(count),
    percent = 100 * proportion
  )

ImportantSensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis asks whether a substantive pattern changes when an important analytical choice changes.

If the interpretation changes substantially under an alternative reasonable treatment, the conclusion is sensitive to that decision. This does not by itself establish which treatment is historically correct.

WarningStatistical scope

This assignment is limited to descriptive missing-data analysis, visualization, recoding, and simple sensitivity analysis.

Do not perform t-tests, regression, Bayesian modeling, multiple imputation, MICE, or other inferential analyses. Do not attempt to reproduce the original Whitehouse et al. or Beheim et al. statistical models from this three-row teaching summary.
