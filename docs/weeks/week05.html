Week 5 — Exploratory Data Analysis and Visualization

Looking directly at data before formal inference

Published

October 5, 2026

Class meetings: 5 & 7 October 2026
Focus: exploratory data analysis · visualization · distributions · comparison
Case study: Kinbank global kinship terminology data
Assessment: Assignment 2 — Visualizing Kinship Diversity with Kinbank

Overview

This week is about learning to look at data before making formal claims about it.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) uses summaries and especially graphics to expose empirical structure: common patterns, unusual observations, possible relationships, and questions worth pursuing. Visualization is therefore not decoration added after analysis. It is part of analysis.

The anthropological case study is Kinbank, a global comparative database of kinship terminology. Its standardized genealogical framework makes cross-linguistic differences systematically comparable (Passmore et al. 2023). Kinbank also illustrates an important caution: languages may share historical relationships, so cross-cultural observations are not necessarily independent (Passmore and Jordan 2020).

NoteThe Week 5 principle

Look first. Model second. Infer carefully.

Use graphics to expose empirical structure before trying to explain why that structure exists. Description is not inference, but inference without description is blind.

Questions

  • What is exploratory data analysis, and how does it differ from confirmatory analysis?
  • Why can numerical summaries alone fail to describe a dataset?
  • How do measurement scales guide the choice of graphic?
  • How do marks and visual channels turn variables into visible patterns?
  • What do bar charts, histograms, density plots, boxplots, scatterplots, and facets reveal?
  • How can ordering, bin width, colour, and faceting change what we see?
  • Why is comparison central to effective visualization?
  • How does Kinbank make culturally specific kin terminologies comparable?
  • Why does shared ancestry complicate cross-cultural comparison?
  • How do we separate exploratory description from inference?

Learning objectives

By the end of Week 5, you should be able to:

  1. explain the purpose of EDA and distinguish exploration from confirmation;
  2. explain why visualization is an analytical tool;
  3. match graphical forms to variable measurement scales;
  4. describe the grammar of graphics in terms of data, aesthetics, geoms, stats, scales, facets, coordinates, and themes;
  5. explain why some visual channels support more accurate comparisons than others;
  6. use bar charts and dot plots for categorical variables;
  7. use histograms, density plots, jitter plots, and boxplots for numerical distributions;
  8. explain how histogram bin width changes apparent structure;
  9. use scatterplots to explore co-variation;
  10. compare distributions across groups and use faceting effectively;
  11. explain graphical integrity and unnecessary visual clutter;
  12. describe Kinbank’s comparative data architecture; and
  13. distinguish descriptive patterns from explanations requiring additional evidence.

This week’s two-class sequence

Class 1 — How visualization makes structure visible

We begin with exploration versus confirmation, Anscombe’s quartet, visual perception, graphical integrity, and the grammar of graphics. We then connect measurement scales to graphical forms and examine categorical and continuous distributions.

Class 2 — Comparison, Kinbank, and anthropological interpretation

We move to relationships, grouped distributions, faceting, high-dimensional EDA, and three comparison strategies: juxtaposition, superposition, and explicit encoding. Kinbank then shows how comparative anthropological data are standardized, visualized, and interpreted.

1. Exploratory and confirmatory analysis

EDA asks what might be going on? It discovers patterns, generates hypotheses, and identifies unusual observations. Confirmatory analysis asks whether a specified formal claim is supported.

Anscombe’s quartet shows why looking matters: datasets can have the same means, correlation, and fitted regression line while displaying very different structures. A numerical summary can therefore be correct and still be an inadequate description.

2. Visualization is part of analysis

A graphic is a designed representation. The analyst decides which variables to show, which marks represent observations, how variables are encoded, how categories are ordered, whether observations are aggregated, how bins are defined, and whether groups are layered or separated.

Effective graphics should make empirical structure easier to judge rather than harder.

Graphical integrity

Two practical principles from the lecture are:

  • Maximize data ink.
  • Minimize chartjunk.

The point is not to make every graphic visually bare. The point is to ensure that design serves the evidence rather than competing with it.

3. Visual perception

Some visual channels are easier to compare accurately than others.

Visual channel Useful for
Spatial position continuous and ordered values
Length / height quantitative comparison
Hue discrete groups
Luminance ordered magnitude
Shape a small number of groups
Area magnitude, but often judged less accurately

This helps explain why aligned bar lengths are usually easier to compare than pie-chart angles or areas.

4. The grammar of graphics

ggplot2 builds plots from explicit components:

  1. Data + aesthetics (aes)
  2. Geoms
  3. Stats
  4. Scales
  5. Facets
  6. Coordinates
  7. Theme
ggplot(data, aes(x = variable)) +
  geom_histogram()

The key question is: How is each variable being made visible?

5. Chart choice begins with measurement scale

Measurement scale Useful summaries Common exploratory graphics
Nominal mode; frequency; proportion bar chart; dot plot
Ordinal median; percentiles; IQR ordered bar; percentile display
Interval / ratio mean/median; SD; variance; IQR; MAD histogram; density; boxplot

Before plotting, ask:

  1. What does one row represent?
  2. What variable am I examining?
  3. What is its measurement scale?
  4. What comparison do I want to make?
  5. Which graphic makes that comparison easiest?

6. Categorical variables

For nominal variables, begin with frequencies or proportions.

ggplot(data, aes(x = category)) +
  geom_bar()

When categories have no natural order, sorting by frequency can reveal dominant and rare categories. Horizontal bars can help with long labels. Dot plots can reduce clutter when many categories must be compared.

Pie charts are usually less effective for precise comparison because angles and areas are harder to compare than aligned lengths.

7. Continuous variables

When examining a numerical distribution, look for location, spread, shape, clusters, gaps, and unusual observations.

Strip or jitter plots

These preserve raw observations and can reveal clusters and gaps.

Histograms

Histograms divide a numerical variable into bins.

ggplot(data, aes(x = value)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5)

Bin width changes the story. Bins that are too wide can erase structure; bins that are too narrow can make random fluctuations look important. Trying several plausible bin widths is part of EDA.

Density plots

Density plots provide a smoothed view of distribution shape.

Boxplots

Boxplots emphasize the median and interquartile range. They are compact and useful for group comparison, but they can hide raw distribution shape, including bimodality.

ImportantNo single plot shows everything

A jitter plot shows raw observations, a histogram emphasizes binned shape, a density plot emphasizes smoothed shape, and a boxplot emphasizes robust location and spread. Compare views rather than treating one representation as definitive.

8. Relationships and group comparisons

Numerical × numerical

Scatterplots make co-variation visible.

ggplot(data, aes(x = x_variable, y = y_variable)) +
  geom_point()

Ask whether the relationship is linear, curved, clustered, dominated by unusual observations, or obscured by overplotting. The lecture introduces LOESS/GAM smoothing and, for large datasets, alpha blending, hexagonal binning, two-dimensional binning, and contour density.

Categorical × continuous

Do not compare only a single point estimate when distributions differ in shape.

ggplot(data, aes(x = group, y = value)) +
  geom_boxplot()

Boxplots compare medians and spreads; violin plots add density shape; ridgeline plots can help when many groups must be compared.

9. Graphics gain power through comparison

The lecture emphasizes three strategies.

Juxtaposition

Place related graphics side by side, including small multiples and facets.

ggplot(data, aes(x = value)) +
  geom_histogram() +
  facet_wrap(~ group)

Superposition

Layer groups in the same coordinate space.

Explicit encoding

Show departure from a baseline or expectation:

[ = - ]

The exploratory sequence is:

  1. Expose patterns with raw graphics.
  2. Model expectations as a baseline.
  3. Show deviations from that expectation.

10. High-dimensional EDA

When variables multiply, pairwise inspection becomes difficult. The lecture introduces scatterplot matrices, cluster heatmaps, and principal component analysis (PCA).

PCA reduces many variables to a smaller number of components. Plotting PC1 and PC2 can reveal major dimensions of variation, while biplots can show observations together with loading vectors. Here the purpose is exploratory: reduce complexity enough to generate visual hypotheses.

11. Kinbank: making kinship terminology analyzable

Kinbank contains 210,903 kin terms from 1,229 spoken and signed languages (Passmore et al. 2023). Its methodological importance lies in its data architecture.

Etic grids reveal emic categories

English categories such as aunt, uncle, or cousin cannot simply be treated as universal. Kinbank instead uses an etic genealogical grid of standardized genealogical positions. Language-specific kin terms are mapped onto those positions.

Researchers can then examine which positions a language merges under one term and which it splits into different terms.

The etic grid is a comparative framework, not a claim that culturally meaningful emic categories are identical across languages.

NoteEDA begins with data architecture

Before interpreting a plot, ask what has been counted, how observations were standardized, what one row represents, and what comparisons the database makes possible.

12. Kinbank as visual evidence

The lecture uses two examples from Passmore et al. (2023).

Parent-term phonology

Density distributions make recurrent cross-linguistic sound patterns visible, including nasal consonants in mother terms and stop consonants in father terms.

Kinship typology and phylogeny

The second example introduces non-independence. Languages can resemble one another because they share ancestry. Treating every society or language as an independent observation can therefore be misleading.

Passmore and Jordan (2020) show that shared ancestry structures variation in kinship terminologies across language families. This is a version of Galton’s Problem.

For Assignment 2, the practical lesson is:

Describe the visible pattern, but do not leap from a visual association to a causal explanation.

13. A practical ANTH418 EDA workflow

Step 1 — Inspect and clean the data

glimpse(data)
names(data)
dim(data)

Check variable meanings, missing values, category labels, and the unit of observation.

Step 2 — Plot univariate distributions

Use bar charts for categories; histograms or density plots for numerical shape; jitter plots for raw observations; and boxplots for compact robust summaries.

Step 3 — Explore co-variation

Use scatterplots for two numerical variables and grouped or faceted distributions when one variable is categorical.

Step 4 — Compare explicitly

Use facets, layers, or departures from a baseline. Choose the comparison design before interpreting the pattern.

Step 5 — Separate description from inference

First state what the visualization shows. Then ask what additional evidence would be needed to explain it.

The workflow is iterative. A plot may reveal a coding problem, missingness, an unexpected subgroup, or a better question and send you back to an earlier step.

14. Assignment 2 — Visualizing Kinship Diversity with Kinbank

Assignment 2 asks you to use the provided Kinbank teaching dataset to conduct an exploratory data analysis of variation in kinship terminology.

The assignment applies the central Week 5 skills:

  • inspect the dataset and identify its observations and variables;
  • use bar charts, histograms, boxplots, and faceted plots where appropriate;
  • treat visualizations as analytical tools rather than final decoration;
  • compare alternative views of the same data;
  • describe principal patterns, differences, and unusual observations;
  • explain what a graphic reveals and what it may conceal;
  • recognize possible historical non-independence among languages; and
  • explore and describe before attempting to explain.

No inferential statistical tests are required.

Practice

Exercise 1 — Inspect before plotting

For the Kinbank teaching dataset, identify the unit of observation, variable types, missing values, and variables useful for comparison.

Exercise 2 — Compare two representations

Create two appropriate visualizations of the same variable. Write 2–3 sentences explaining what each representation makes easier to see and what it hides.

Exercise 3 — Facet a comparison

Create a faceted plot for a meaningful grouping variable. Ask whether groups differ in location, spread, shape, or unusual observations. Describe only what is visible.

Exercise 4 — Description versus explanation

For one pattern, write:

  1. one sentence of description;
  2. one possible hypothesis that could explain it; and
  3. one sentence explaining why the visualization alone does not test that hypothesis.

Readings

Required: Beecham

Chapter 3, Visualization Fundamentals (pp. 41–71) and Chapter 4, Exploratory Data Analysis (pp. 73–96).

Focus on effective data graphics, grammar of graphics, marks and visual channels, chart choice, continuous and categorical distributions, comparison, and the role of models as exploratory baselines.

Required: Passmore et al.

Read Kinbank as both an anthropological study and a lesson in data architecture. Focus on how the standardized etic genealogical framework makes cross-linguistic comparison possible, the database’s scale, and the examples showing structured variation.

Required: Thulin

Focus on EDA as question asking, visual exploration, identifying unusual observations, and generating hypotheses. Review distribution graphics, grouped comparisons, and multivariate visualization insofar as they reinforce the lecture.

Wickham, Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Grolemund

Use the visualization material as the practical ggplot2 companion: aesthetic mappings, geoms, layers, distributions, relationships, and facets.

Supplementary readings

The supplementary readings deepen themes already present in the lecture. Tukey provides the foundational exploratory perspective; Tufte develops graphical integrity; Cumming emphasizes location, spread, shape, and the danger of relying on one picture; Peng and Downey reinforce iterative exploratory workflows; Castro provides broader visualization context; and Passmore and Jordan (2020) provides the key caution about phylogenetic non-independence.

Week 5 checklist

Takeaways

  1. EDA is analytical, not decorative.
  2. Numerical summaries can conceal structure; always look at the data.
  3. Chart choice follows measurement scale and analytical purpose.
  4. Different graphics reveal different properties of the same distribution.
  5. Comparison gives graphics analytical power.
  6. Good visualization makes evidence easier to judge.
  7. Kinbank demonstrates that data architecture determines what cross-cultural comparison becomes possible.
  8. Cross-cultural observations may be historically related rather than independent.
  9. Description comes before explanation.
  10. Look first. Model second. Infer carefully.
