Week 7 — Probability, Sampling, and Sampling Distributions

Why This Week Matters

Quantitative anthropologists almost never observe an entire population. Archaeologists cannot excavate every square meter, date every surviving sample, or recover everything that once existed. Week 7 asks how we can reason responsibly from partial observations.

Sampling creates uncertainty in two different ways. Even a well-designed random sample varies by chance from one sample to another: sampling variability. A sampling frame or selection process can also systematically overrepresent some parts of a population and underrepresent others: sampling bias. A larger sample can reduce the first problem without necessarily correcting the second.

Assignment 4 applies these ideas to archaeological radiocarbon determinations from the p3k14c project.

What You Should Understand

By the end of Week 7, you should be able to:

  1. explain probability as a long-run relative frequency;
  2. distinguish population/sample and parameter/statistic;
  3. explain a sampling frame and why it matters;
  4. distinguish probability from nonprobability sampling;
  5. explain sampling with versus without replacement;
  6. explain sampling variability and a sampling distribution;
  7. distinguish standard deviation from standard error and explain the effect of sample size;
  8. state the Central Limit Theorem at an introductory level and avoid common misconceptions;
  9. distinguish sampling variability from sampling bias;
  10. explain why a large sample can be precise but unrepresentative; and
  11. apply these ideas to radiocarbon databases and p3k14c.

1. Probability as Long-Run Frequency

Probability gives us a language for uncertainty. In the frequentist interpretation used here, probability is understood through repeated trials. A fair coin has probability 0.5 of landing heads, but ten flips need not contain exactly five heads. Short runs fluctuate.

As the number of repeated trials becomes very large, the observed proportion tends to stabilize near the underlying probability.

Random does not mean patternless. Short runs can fluctuate substantially even when the long-run probability is stable.

This prepares us for sampling: a random sample can differ noticeably from its population simply by chance.

2. Population, Sample, Parameter, and Statistic

Population world Sample world
Population — complete target universe Sample — observed subset
Parameter — numerical property of the population Statistic — numerical property calculated from a sample
Population mean: () Sample mean: ({x})
Population SD: () Sample SD: (s)
Population proportion: (P) Sample proportion: (p)

A statistic estimates a parameter, but it is not the parameter itself. Different random samples from the same population usually produce somewhat different statistics.

3. The Sampling Frame

The target population is what we want to make claims about. The sampling frame is the operational set of cases from which observations can actually be selected: a list, database, register, site inventory, survey grid, or other accessible collection.

A sample directly represents the frame from which it was selected, not automatically every population we would like it to represent.

If part of the target population is absent from the frame, random selection cannot recover what is missing.

Target population → Sampling frame → Sample → Statistic

4. Probability and Nonprobability Sampling

Probability sampling

Units have known, non-zero probabilities of selection. Designs introduced in the lecture include simple random, systematic, stratified, and cluster/PPS sampling. Probability sampling matters because the selection process is explicitly modeled and, under appropriate conditions, sampling uncertainty can be quantified.

Nonprobability sampling

Purposive, quota, theoretical, snowball/RDS, and convenience sampling can be appropriate for particular research goals, especially qualitative depth, pilot research, expert knowledge, and hard-to-reach populations.

The limitation is inferential: if selection probabilities are unknown, ordinary probability-sampling logic cannot simply be used to estimate sampling error for population parameters.

5. Sampling With and Without Replacement

Without replacement: once selected, a case cannot be selected again in the same sample.

With replacement: after selection, the case returns to the available pool and can be selected again.

In R:

sample(x, size = 25, replace = TRUE)

Assignment 4 uses sampling with replacement to simulate repeated random sampling from a fixed teaching population. This does not mean archaeologists literally excavate the same object repeatedly. It creates a repeated-sampling model in which each draw comes from the same reference distribution.

6. Sampling Variability

Draw several independent random samples of the same size and calculate a mean for each:

[ {x}_1, {x}_2, {x}_3, {x}_4, {x}_5 ]

They will usually differ. This is sampling variability: a statistic changes from one random sample to another because different observations happen to be selected.

Cumming calls this the “dance of the means.” Sampling variability is not evidence that random sampling failed; it is an unavoidable consequence of observing only part of a variable population.

7. Sampling Distributions

Imagine repeating the sampling process many times. For each repetition:

  1. draw a random sample of size (n);
  2. calculate its mean;
  3. save that mean; and
  4. repeat.

The collection of sample means forms the sampling distribution of the sample mean.

A data distribution describes individual observations. A sampling distribution describes a statistic across repeated samples.

In Assignment 4, individual conventional radiocarbon ages form the distribution of age; 1,000 means from 1,000 repeated samples form an empirical sampling distribution of the mean.

8. Sample Size and Standard Error

Larger random samples tend to produce more stable estimates:

small (n) → more variable sample means → wider sampling distribution

large (n) → less variable sample means → narrower sampling distribution

The standard error (SE) describes the spread of a sampling distribution. For a sample mean:

[ SE_{{x}}= ]

When () is unknown, (s/) is commonly used as an estimate.

Because SE changes with the square root of (n), doubling sample size does not halve SE. Approximately four times the sample size is required to halve the standard error.

Standard deviation versus standard error

SD describes variability among individual observations.

SE describes variability of a statistic across repeated samples.

9. The Central Limit Theorem

At an introductory level, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) tells us that, under appropriate conditions, the sampling distribution of a mean tends toward a normal distribution as sample size increases.

The important object is the sampling distribution, not the raw observations.

The CLT does not mean that raw data become normal when (n) is large, that every population must be normal, or that (n ) guarantees normality in every situation. The lecture emphasizes independence and finite variance, and convergence also depends on features such as skewness and tail behavior.

For ANTH418, the main importance of the CLT is conceptual: it helps explain why sampling distributions can become predictable enough to support statistical inference.

10. Sampling Variability Is Not Sampling Bias

Sampling variability Sampling bias
Random fluctuation Systematic distortion
Occurs even with good random sampling Arises from the frame or selection process
Different samples give different statistics Some parts are systematically over- or underrepresented
Generally decreases as (n) increases Does not necessarily disappear as (n) increases
Reflected in SE Not repaired simply by obtaining a smaller SE

A very large biased sample can provide a highly precise estimate of the wrong target quantity.

A small SE means repeated samples from the same sampling process would give similar estimates. It does not prove that the process represents the intended target population.

11. Sample Size versus Sample Quality

The Literary Digest example in the lecture demonstrates why a huge sample can perform badly when its sampling frame systematically underrepresents important groups.

Increasing (n) helps with random sampling variability. It does not automatically correct coverage bias, selection bias, nonresponse bias, geographic imbalance, or research-history bias.

Before asking “How large is the sample?”, also ask:

How did these observations become eligible to enter the sample?

12. Archaeological Application: Dates-as-Data

Radiocarbon databases are large but strongly filtered collections:

Past human activity → datable material → preservation → archaeological discovery → sample selection → laboratory dating → database inclusion → observed radiocarbon distribution

Filters include preservation, decay and erosion as well as excavation history, research interests, funding, access, choices about what to date, and database compilation.

A radiocarbon database is not a census of past human activity.

A raw distribution of dates can reflect both prehistoric processes and modern research behavior.

13. Bird et al. (2022) and p3k14c

Bird et al. assembled and standardized a very large global database of archaeological radiocarbon dates and evaluated dimensions of data quality and sampling coverage.

For Week 7, the crucial lesson is not simply that p3k14c is large. It is that large databases can remain unevenly sampled.

The lecture highlights geographic imbalance and Bird et al.’s comparison of radiocarbon-date density with archaeological-site density. Some regions have disproportionately many dates relative to sites; others have relatively few.

This demonstrates the same statistical principle:

Large (n) does not guarantee representativeness.

14. Reading Guide

Bernard — Probability Sampling

Focus on target populations, sampling frames, random selection, representativeness, sampling bias, and the scope of legitimate generalization. The key lesson is that sampling design determines the scope of inference.

Cumming — The Normal Distribution and Sampling

Focus on population parameters versus sample statistics, the “dance of the means,” the “mean heap,” sampling distributions, standard error, the relationship between (n) and SE, and the CLT.

The visual intuition is especially important: repeated sample means “dance,” but larger samples produce a narrower dance.

Bird et al. — p3k14c

Read the database as a sampling problem, not simply a source of dates. Ask where the dates came from, how they were assembled and standardized, whether geographic coverage is even, how sampling intensity is evaluated, and how research history may affect the observed distribution.

Thulin — Recommended Computational Support

Use Thulin for simulation in R: repeated simulation, random sampling, sample(), sampling with replacement, set.seed(), reproducibility, and the effect of changing sample size. Later inferential procedures in the chapter are not required for Assignment 4.

15. Assignment 4 Connection

The Southern California teaching file contains 984 radiocarbon determinations and is treated as a known reference population for the exercise.

Experiment 1: Change sample size

Repeated random samples at different (n) values demonstrate:

larger (n) → less sampling variability → smaller SE → greater precision

Experiment 2: Change the sampling frame

The exercise then restricts the frame geographically. Sampling remains random within that restricted frame, but increasing (n) makes estimates increasingly stable around the restricted-frame mean. It does not force them toward the mean of the full teaching population.

Therefore:

larger (n) can reduce variability without eliminating bias

This is the central lesson of Assignment 4.

16. Common Misunderstandings

  • “A random sample should look exactly like the population.” No. Random samples fluctuate.
  • “A statistic is the true population value.” No. A statistic estimates a parameter.
  • “A sampling distribution is the histogram of observations in my sample.” No. It is a distribution of a statistic across repeated samples.
  • “SD and SE are the same.” No. SD describes observations; SE describes sampling variability of a statistic.
  • “Doubling (n) halves SE.” No. Roughly four times (n) is needed.
  • “The CLT says large datasets become normal.” No. It concerns sampling distributions of statistics under appropriate conditions.
  • “(n=30) is always enough.” No. There is no universal guarantee.
  • “A huge sample must be representative.” No. Large biased samples can be very precise.
  • “Random sampling fixes a bad frame.” No. Random selection only operates on cases available in the frame.
  • “A large radiocarbon database directly represents prehistoric population.” Not automatically; preservation and research processes filter it.

18. Core Takeaway

Week 7 can be summarized as a chain:

Population → Sampling frame → Sample → Statistic → Repeated samples → Sampling distribution → Standard error → Inference

But statistical precision is only one part of defensible inference.

Larger random samples generally reduce sampling variability and increase precision, but increasing sample size does not correct a systematically biased sampling frame.

In archaeology, this matters especially because every observed dataset has already been filtered by preservation, recovery, research history, selection, and recording practices.
