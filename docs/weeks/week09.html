Week 9 — Comparing Two Groups: t-tests

Design → quantify the mean difference → scale by uncertainty → estimate magnitude → interpret archaeologically

Overview

This week extends Week 8 from inference about an estimate or null model to a direct comparison between two groups. Two sample means almost never match exactly. The important questions are: How large is the observed difference? How uncertain is it? How compatible is it with a population difference of zero? And what does it mean archaeologically?

The lecture follows one connected sequence:

independent vs. paired designs → standard error of a mean difference → t statistic and t distribution → degrees of freedom → Welch’s t-test → assumptions and robustness → t-based CI → raw and standardized effect magnitude → Student vs. Welch → parametric vs. rank-based alternatives → Tell Tweini interpretation

The order matters. Design determines what counts as independent information; design and variance assumptions determine the standard error; the standard error makes the t statistic interpretable; confidence intervals and effect estimates describe magnitude and uncertainty; interpretation then returns the statistical result to archaeology.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish independent-samples from paired-samples designs;
  • explain why the observational unit and independence must be established before choosing a test;
  • calculate and interpret a difference between two means;
  • explain the standard error of a mean difference;
  • interpret the t statistic as a difference scaled by uncertainty;
  • explain the role of the t distribution and degrees of freedom;
  • distinguish Student’s pooled and Welch’s unpooled independent-samples t-tests;
  • explain why Welch’s t-test is a useful default for independent groups;
  • evaluate assumptions using design knowledge, EDA, and diagnostics;
  • interpret a 95% t-based confidence interval for a mean difference;
  • distinguish raw from standardized effect magnitude;
  • distinguish t-tests from Mann–Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank alternatives;
  • use R to conduct and report a two-group comparison;
  • interpret the Tell Tweini result as archaeological evidence rather than as a p value alone.

Required Readings

Perkins, M. A. (2026). Using R for introductory statistics in social sciences. Springer. Chapter 7, “Group Comparisons with Scale Dependent Variables,” pp. 85–111.

Fuller, B. T., Riehl, S., Linseele, V., Marinova, E., De Cupere, B., Bretschneider, J., Richards, M. P., & Van Neer, W. (2024). Agropastoral and dietary practices of the northern Levant facing Late Holocene climate and environmental change: Isotopic analysis of plants, animals and humans from Bronze to Iron Age Tell Tweini. PLOS ONE, 19(6), e0301775. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0301775

Fuller et al. provide the archaeological case. Their statistical protocol used Shapiro–Wilk tests first; datasets treated as normal were followed by Levene’s test and independent-samples t-tests, while non-normal datasets were compared with Mann–Whitney U tests.

Movement I — Design → Quantify the Mean Difference

1. Independent vs. paired designs

Independent samples contain distinct observations. An observation in Group 1 has no matched partner in Group 2. Sample sizes may differ. Examples include ceramic rim diameters from two contexts, house areas from two periods, and isotope measurements from specimens in two archaeological phases. The Tell Tweini Middle Bronze Age and Late Bronze Age wild ryegrass specimens are independent: no seed in one phase is the same specimen measured again in the other phase.

Paired samples contain linked observations. Each value in one condition has a meaningful partner in the other. Examples include left versus right humeri from the same skeleton, two observers measuring the same bones, or the same unit measured before and after an intervention.

For each pair:

\[D_i=X_{1i}-X_{2i}\]

The paired test analyzes these difference scores. Pairing can subtract background variation and increase precision.

ImportantDesign comes first

Independent versus paired is not chosen from a software menu. It is determined by the way observations were generated and by what constitutes an independent archaeological unit.

2. The mean difference

For independent groups:

\[\widehat{\Delta}=\bar X_1-\bar X_2\]

For paired data:

\[\widehat{\Delta}=\bar D\]

The sign depends on the order of subtraction. Assignment 6 defines the effect as Middle Bronze Age − Late Bronze Age, so always keep that direction visible when interpreting the result.

3. Standard error of the mean difference

The difference between sample means varies from sample to sample. Its uncertainty is summarized by the standard error of the difference.

Student’s equal-variance test pools the variances:

\[s_p^2=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}\]

\[SE_{\text{Student}}=\sqrt{s_p^2\left(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}\right)}\]

Welch’s test keeps the variances separate:

\[SE_{\text{Welch}}=\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}\]

For paired samples:

\[SE_{\bar D}=\frac{s_D}{\sqrt N}\]

Key idea: independent, paired, Student, and Welch procedures differ chiefly in how they represent uncertainty.

Movement II — Difference → t Statistic → Degrees of Freedom

4. The t statistic

For the usual zero-difference null model:

\[H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\]

and

\[t=\frac{(\bar X_1-\bar X_2)-0}{SE_{\text{diff}}}\]

The t statistic is a difference-to-uncertainty ratio. A large absolute value means that the observed difference is far from zero relative to its estimated sampling variability. It is not an effect size: it changes with both effect magnitude and precision.

5. The t distribution

The t distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped but has heavier tails than the normal distribution, especially with small samples. Population variability is unknown and must be estimated from sample data; the heavier tails represent that extra uncertainty. As degrees of freedom increase, the t distribution approaches the standard normal distribution.

6. Degrees of freedom

Student independent samples:

\[df=n_1+n_2-2\]

Paired samples:

\[df=N_{\text{pairs}}-1\]

Welch uses the Welch–Satterthwaite approximation:

\[df=\frac{\left(s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}\]

Welch degrees of freedom can be fractional. Lower degrees of freedom imply heavier tails and greater uncertainty.

7. Welch’s t-test

Welch’s procedure uses separate group variances in the standard error and adjusts degrees of freedom. When sample sizes and variances are similar, Student and Welch are usually very close. When they differ, Welch avoids imposing an inappropriate common-variance model.

In R, Welch is the default:

t.test(y ~ group, data = dat)

The pooled Student test is requested explicitly:

t.test(y ~ group, data = dat, var.equal = TRUE)

Assignment 6 therefore uses Welch as the primary teaching analysis and retains Student’s pooled test as a secondary, publication-aligned comparison.

8. Assumptions and robustness

A valid comparison requires more than running t.test().

Measurement. The outcome should be quantitative and meaningfully summarized by a mean.

Independence. Observations must represent independent information for an independent-samples test. This is an archaeological and sampling question, not something a normality test can diagnose. Multiple measurements from one specimen or strongly clustered observations can create pseudoreplication.

Normality. Inspect raw-data plots, histograms/density plots, boxplots, and Q–Q plots. Shapiro–Wilk can supplement these checks but should not replace them. t-tests are often reasonably robust to modest departures from normality, especially without severe outliers.

Variance. Equal population variance is an assumption of Student’s pooled test. Welch relaxes it.

Fuller et al. (2024) used Shapiro–Wilk, then Levene plus an independent-samples t-test for datasets treated as normal, and Mann–Whitney U for non-normal datasets. Assignment 6 reproduces the logic of diagnostics while not requiring a preliminary variance test to choose Welch.

Movement III — Estimate Magnitude → Choose the Comparison → Interpret

9. t-based confidence interval

A confidence interval estimates the population mean difference:

\[CI_{95\%}=\widehat{\Delta}\pm t_{\text{crit}}SE_{\text{diff}}\]

Read it for both precision and magnitude. A narrow interval is more precise than a wide one. Its location shows which differences remain compatible with the data and model. Do not reduce a CI to “crosses zero / does not cross zero.” For archaeology, the raw-unit interval is especially valuable because it remains on the substantive measurement scale.

10. Raw and standardized effect magnitude

The raw mean difference remains in the original archaeological units. At Tell Tweini, Δ¹³C is measured in per mil (‰). This is the most direct scale for connecting the comparison to crop water status.

Cohen’s d expresses the mean difference in pooled within-group standard-deviation units:

\[d=\frac{\bar X_1-\bar X_2}{s_p}\]

Hedges’ g applies a small-sample correction and is less biased in small samples.

Measure Main question
Raw mean difference How much did the measured quantity differ in its original units?
Cohen’s d / Hedges’ g How large is the difference relative to within-group variability?

Do not let conventional labels such as “small,” “medium,” or “large” replace archaeological interpretation.

11. Student vs. Welch

The two procedures test the same broad zero-difference null model but represent uncertainty differently.

Student Welch
Pools the two variances Keeps variances separate
Assumes equal population variances Does not require equal population variances
\(df=n_1+n_2-2\) Welch–Satterthwaite \(df\)
Use when a common-variance model is justified Useful independent-samples default

The point is not to hunt for whichever test gives the preferred p value. The point is to make the variance model explicit.

12. Parametric vs. rank-based alternatives

Mann–Whitney U is a rank-based comparison for independent groups. Wilcoxon signed-rank is a rank-based procedure for paired differences. Permutation tests construct an empirical null distribution by rearranging labels or signs under an exchangeability assumption.

These are not simply interchangeable “safer t-tests.” They operate on ranks or resampled arrangements and may target different features of distributions. Test choice should follow the research question, measurement scale, design, distribution, and assumptions—not a ritual decision tree based on one diagnostic p value.

Case Study — Tell Tweini

Archaeological setting

Tell Tweini, ancient Gibala, lies on the Syrian Mediterranean coast near modern Jableh. Fuller et al. (2024) analyze a long sequence spanning the Early Bronze Age through the Iron Age and crossing the 4.2 ka BP and 3.2 ka BP climatic perturbations. Their dataset combines stable isotope evidence from plants, animals, and humans to investigate water availability, manuring, animal management, and human diet.

The published study reports a large isotopic dataset based on plants (\(n=410\)), animals with acceptable collagen results (\(n=210\)), and humans with acceptable collagen results (\(n=16\)). Each evidence stream has a different observational unit, sample size, and inferential link. This is why comparison begins with design rather than with a test button.

Assignment 6 — wild ryegrass Δ¹³C

Assignment 6 focuses on source-derived Δ¹³C values for Lolium cf. multiflorum:

  • Middle Bronze Age: \(n=10\);
  • Late Bronze Age: \(n=10\).

The effect is defined as:

Middle Bronze Age − Late Bronze Age

The assignment’s central question is:

How large is the difference in wild ryegrass Δ¹³C between the Middle Bronze Age and Late Bronze Age at Tell Tweini, how uncertain is that difference, and what does a Welch two-sample t-test contribute to its interpretation?

This is an independent-groups design. The primary ANTH418 analysis is Welch’s t-test, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval and effect magnitude. The equal-variance Student test is retained for comparison with the publication-aligned approach.

Fuller et al. (2024) report that wild ryegrass Δ¹³C decreases from the Early/Middle Bronze Age toward the Late Bronze/Iron Age and that the Middle-to-Late Bronze Age difference is statistically significant with an independent-samples t-test (p = .015). They interpret the change as consistent with ryegrass growing with a crop under reduced moisture availability. Because ryegrass is a weed that may occur with different crops, that interpretation must also consider crop associations, chronology, environmental context, and the broader archaeobotanical evidence.

What the comparison can establish

It can quantify the direction and raw magnitude of the Δ¹³C difference, the uncertainty around it, and its compatibility with a zero-difference null model under the statistical assumptions.

It cannot by itself establish that climate change caused the difference, identify the crop with which every ryegrass specimen grew, guarantee representativeness of all agriculture in each period, or turn statistical significance into archaeological importance.

The statistical result supports interpretation; it is not the interpretation.

R Workflow for Week 9

Follow this chain:

Identify design → visualize and describe → estimate the raw difference → consider assumptions → Welch t-test + 95% CI → effect magnitude → compare with pooled Student t → interpret archaeologically

A minimal workflow is:

library(tidyverse)

dat <- read_csv("data/tell_tweini_ttest.csv",
                show_col_types = FALSE)

dat |>
  group_by(period) |>
  summarise(
    n = n(),
    mean = mean(isotope_value),
    sd = sd(isotope_value),
    median = median(isotope_value),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

ggplot(dat, aes(x = period, y = isotope_value)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.08, alpha = 0.7) +
  theme_minimal()

# Primary analysis: Welch
t.test(isotope_value ~ period, data = dat)

# Publication-aligned pooled comparison
t.test(isotope_value ~ period, data = dat,
       var.equal = TRUE)

For the assignment, also inspect group distributions and Shapiro–Wilk diagnostics. Report the group means and SDs, raw mean difference and direction, 95% CI, t, degrees of freedom, p, standardized effect magnitude, and archaeological interpretation.

Key Distinctions to Remember

Distinction Remember
Independent vs. paired Distinct observations vs. meaningful one-to-one pairing
Difference vs. t Raw effect vs. effect scaled by standard error
SD vs. SE Variation among observations vs. uncertainty of an estimate
Student vs. Welch Pooled equal variance vs. separate variances and adjusted df
p value vs. effect magnitude Compatibility with a null model vs. how much difference
Raw vs. standardized effect Archaeological units vs. SD units
Parametric vs. rank-based Mean-based model vs. rank-based comparison
Statistical vs. archaeological inference Quantitative comparison vs. substantive explanation

Study Questions

  1. Why must you decide whether a design is independent or paired before calculating a standard error?
  2. Why do two sample means almost never match exactly?
  3. What does \(SE_{\text{diff}}\) represent?
  4. What are the numerator and denominator of the usual two-sample t statistic?
  5. Why does the t distribution have heavier tails at low degrees of freedom?
  6. Why can Welch’s degrees of freedom be fractional?
  7. What assumption does Student’s pooled test make that Welch’s test relaxes?
  8. Why is independence especially difficult to justify in archaeological datasets?
  9. Why should a confidence interval be interpreted for magnitude and precision rather than only by whether it contains zero?
  10. Why is a raw Δ¹³C difference more archaeologically legible than Cohen’s d alone?
  11. Why are Mann–Whitney U and a two-sample t-test not simply two versions of the same test?
  12. In Assignment 6, why are Middle Bronze Age and Late Bronze Age ryegrass values independent rather than paired?
  13. Why is Welch the primary ANTH418 analysis while the pooled Student test is still useful as a comparison?
  14. What does the Tell Tweini statistical comparison contribute to the archaeological interpretation, and what causal claims remain beyond the test?

Take-Home Message

A two-group comparison is not a single button labeled t-test. It is a chain of reasoning:

design → observed difference → uncertainty → t and degrees of freedom → confidence interval → effect magnitude → method comparison → archaeological interpretation

For independent groups, Welch’s t-test is a strong default because it does not require equal population variances. But no choice of t-test can repair a poorly defined observational unit, non-independent evidence, biased sampling, or weak archaeological interpretation. Report the raw difference, uncertainty, test result, and effect magnitude together, and then return to the archaeological question.
