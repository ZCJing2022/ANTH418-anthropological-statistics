Week 4 — Measures of Central Tendency and Dispersion

Describe the center, measure the spread, then interpret the distribution

Published

September 28, 2026

Class meetings: 28 & 30 September 2026
Focus: descriptive statistics · central tendency · dispersion · distribution shape · visualization
Practice dataset: Knipper et al. 2020 human stable-isotope data
Assessment: Assignment 1 — Bronze Age Diet and Farming at La Bastida and Gatas

Overview

Week 3 asked how concepts become variables and how research design limits inference. Week 4 asks what to do once a defensible numerical variable has been measured:

How can we describe a distribution without losing the pattern that matters?

Raw observations rarely explain themselves. A list of isotope values, ceramic dimensions, fossil measurements, or survey scores may contain important structure, but that structure is difficult to see until the observations are organized, visualized, and summarized. The Week 4 lecture therefore begins with a simple sequence:

raw observations → summary numbers → empirical pattern → interpretation

Descriptive statistics do not explain why a pattern exists. They make the empirical pattern visible enough that an explanation can later be evaluated.

Two questions organize the entire week:

  1. Central tendency: Where is the distribution centered? What value is typical?
  2. Dispersion / variability: How widely are observations spread around that center?

A center without a measure of spread is incomplete. Two archaeological samples can have the same mean and radically different internal variation. Conversely, two samples can have different means but substantial overlap. This is why the Week 4 lecture treats central tendency and dispersion as a pair rather than as separate topics.

Carlson makes the archaeological version of the same point: the first step in analyzing new data is to look at the data, using numerical summaries, tables, and graphs to identify structure and possible errors before more elaborate analysis begins (Carlson 2017). Banning similarly treats descriptive statistics as a way to summarize both where typical values lie and how “clumped” or dispersed archaeological observations are (Banning 2020).

NoteThe Week 4 principle

Describe before explaining.

A defensible descriptive analysis tells the reader:

  • what was measured;
  • what one observation represents;
  • where the distribution is centered;
  • how much it varies;
  • what its shape looks like; and
  • which observations are unusual.

Only then should we ask why the pattern may exist.

Why descriptive statistics come first

Raw tables do not explain themselves

A dataset is a collection of observations, not an interpretation.

Carlson emphasizes that archaeological datasets are commonly organized as rectangular tables in which each row represents an observation and each column represents a measurement or attribute of that observation (Carlson 2017). The meaning of a row can change from dataset to dataset: one row might be a survey grid square, a site, a grave, a ceramic sherd, a stone flake, or a human individual.

That means the first questions are not mathematical:

What does one row represent? What does each column represent? How was each variable measured?

Only after those questions are answered do numerical summaries become meaningful.

ImportantA statistic summarizes observations; it does not replace them

Reducing 69 isotope measurements to a mean is useful because it compresses information.

But compression always loses detail.

The goal is therefore not to find one “best number” and discard the distribution. The goal is to combine a small set of summaries with appropriate visualizations so that the center, spread, shape, and unusual observations remain visible.

Descriptive versus inferential statistics

Descriptive statistics summarize the observations actually present in a dataset.

Examples include:

  • counts and proportions;
  • mean and median;
  • range and IQR;
  • variance and standard deviation;
  • histograms and boxplots.

Inferential statistics use sample information to make uncertainty-aware statements about a larger population or data-generating process.

Assignment 1 is explicitly a descriptive assignment. No inferential statistical test is required (Knipper et al. 2020).

WarningThe Assignment 1 archaeological sample is not the whole El Argar population

The teaching file contains 69 evaluable human observations: 48 from La Bastida and 21 from Gatas.

Those people are an archaeological sample preserved, excavated, sampled, and successfully analyzed under particular conditions. Describing these observations does not by itself justify population-wide claims about every person who lived at either settlement—or about El Argar society as a whole.

Measurement first: what summaries are legitimate?

Week 3 introduced the NOIR framework. Week 4 uses it to decide which summaries are substantively meaningful.

Scale Information preserved Appropriate center Useful spread summaries
Nominal category identity only mode counts, proportions
Ordinal category identity + rank median, mode IQR, range
Interval ordered values + equal intervals mean, median SD, variance, IQR, range
Ratio interval properties + meaningful zero mean, median SD, variance, IQR, range, CV

Perkins uses NOIR as a mnemonic for nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio measurement and stresses that a variable’s mathematical treatment depends on what its values actually mean (Perkins 2026).

A mean can be calculable and meaningless

Suppose an artifact-material variable is coded:

Ceramic = 1
Lithic  = 2
Bone    = 3

R can calculate the arithmetic mean of those numbers.

But a “mean artifact material” of 2.13 has no archaeological interpretation because the numbers are merely labels. The distance from ceramic to lithic is not a measured quantitative interval.

NoteNumeric storage is not quantitative meaning

A variable can be stored as numbers in R and still be nominal.

Always distinguish:

  • how R stores the column, from
  • what the measurements mean.

What scale are δ13C and δ15N?

For Assignment 1, human bone-collagen (^{13}) and (^{15}) values are best treated as interval-like measurements for descriptive purposes.

Differences between values are meaningful, but (0‰) is a reference point relative to an isotope standard—not a true absence of carbon or nitrogen isotope composition. Therefore:

  • means and medians are meaningful;
  • SD, variance, IQR, and range are meaningful;
  • ratios such as “twice the δ15N value” are not meaningful; and
  • CV is not appropriate for the delta values in Assignment 1.
ImportantWhy this matters

Week 4 introduces the coefficient of variation because it is important in anthropology.

But learning a statistic does not mean using it everywhere.

Assignment 1 requires SD, IQR, minimum, maximum, and range—not CV—because the measurement scale and research question do not justify CV for these delta isotope values.

Section 1 — Locating the center

Central tendency summarizes “typical”

A measure of central tendency reduces a distribution to a value intended to represent its center.

The three classical centers are:

Measure Definition Particularly useful when
Mode most frequently occurring value/category nominal data; discrete peaks; multimodality
Median 50th percentile ordinal data; skewed data; outliers
Mean arithmetic average approximately symmetric interval/ratio data
Trimmed mean mean after removing equal percentages from both tails continuous data with influential extremes

Choosing a center is a methodological decision, not a mechanical habit.

The mode: the most frequent value

The mode is the value or category occurring most often.

For categorical variables it may be the only meaningful measure of central tendency.

For continuous archaeological measurements, an exact numerical mode is often less useful because repeated values may depend on measurement precision and rounding. However, modal peaks in a frequency distribution can be archaeologically informative.

The lecture uses standardized stone weights from Mohenjo-Daro as an example: repeated peaks can reveal patterned size classes rather than one smooth continuous distribution.

Unimodal, bimodal, and multimodal

A distribution can have:

  • one dominant peak — unimodal;
  • two dominant peaks — bimodal; or
  • several peaks — multimodal.

Multiple peaks may reflect real subgroups, different production conventions, different populations, or a mixture of processes. They may also arise from small samples or arbitrary histogram bins.

WarningA histogram peak is not automatically a statistical mode

Changing histogram bin width can change the apparent number and location of peaks.

When multimodality matters substantively, inspect the raw values and test whether the apparent structure is robust to reasonable choices of binning.

The median: the positional center

The median is the middle value after observations are ordered.

  • 50% of observations lie at or below it.
  • 50% lie at or above it.

For an odd number of observations, the median is the middle observation.

For an even number, it is conventionally the average of the two middle observations.

The important property is resistance: the median depends on order, not on how far extreme observations lie from the middle.

x <- c(3, 5, 7, 7, 8)
x_extreme <- c(3, 5, 7, 7, 80)

tibble(
  dataset = c("Original", "One extreme value"),
  mean = c(mean(x), mean(x_extreme)),
  median = c(median(x), median(x_extreme))
)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  dataset            mean median
  <chr>             <dbl>  <dbl>
1 Original            6        7
2 One extreme value  20.4      7

The extreme value moves the mean much more than the median.

Dressler uses this contrast to show why mean, median, and mode can converge in an approximately symmetric distribution but diverge when a distribution has a long tail (Dressler 2015).

The arithmetic mean: the balance point

For a sample of (n) observations:

[ {x} = ]

The mean uses every observation.

It can be understood as the distribution’s balance point or center of gravity. If each deviation from the mean is calculated,

[ x_i - {x}, ]

then the deviations sum to zero:

[ _{i=1}^{n}(x_i-{x})=0. ]

This balance property makes the mean fundamental to variance, standard deviation, regression, ANOVA, and many later statistical methods.

Pivitera describes the mean as a balance point rather than necessarily the literal middle observation (Privitera 2024). Dressler similarly presents the mean as a compact model or “best guess” for a value drawn from a roughly symmetric distribution (Dressler 2015).

WarningThe mean is efficient but not resistant

Because every observation contributes to the mean, an extreme observation can pull the balance point toward the tail.

That sensitivity is not a defect to be hidden. It is information about the distribution that should be examined.

The trimmed mean: a robust compromise

A trimmed mean removes the same proportion of the lowest and highest observations before calculating the mean.

Common trims include 5%, 10%, or 20%.

In R:

x_extreme <- c(3, 5, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 80)

mean(x_extreme)
[1] 15.2
mean(x_extreme, trim = 0.10)
[1] 8.625
median(x_extreme)
[1] 8.5

Banning notes that trimmed means can be useful for skewed archaeological distributions or samples containing suspected extremes because they retain much of the data while reducing tail influence (Banning 2020).

A trimmed mean is not required for Assignment 1, but it helps clarify why “center” is not one universal concept.

Section 2 — Quantifying the spread

Why dispersion matters

Suppose two assemblages both have mean artifact length = 50 mm.

One contains measurements tightly concentrated between 48 and 52 mm.

The other ranges from 20 to 80 mm.

The means are identical, but the archaeological patterns are not.

Pivitera emphasizes that variability widens the lens from the center to the structure of the entire distribution (Privitera 2024). Dressler puts the same idea in practical terms: after using a mean as a guess, the next question is how wrong that guess is likely to be (Dressler 2015).

The range

The range is:

[ =-. ]

It is easy to understand, but it depends on only two observations.

x <- c(10, 11, 12, 13, 14)
x_outlier <- c(10, 11, 12, 13, 100)

c(
  range_original = max(x) - min(x),
  range_with_outlier = max(x_outlier) - min(x_outlier)
)
    range_original range_with_outlier 
                 4                 90

The range is therefore fragile:

  • it is highly sensitive to extreme observations;
  • it tends to increase as sample size increases; and
  • it says nothing about how observations are distributed between the minimum and maximum.

Banning makes the archaeological implication explicit: because the range depends only on the two extremes, it is usually a poor stand-alone description of variability (Banning 2020).

Percentiles and quartiles

A percentile is the value below which a specified percentage of observations lies.

Quartiles divide an ordered distribution into four parts:

  • (Q_1): 25th percentile;
  • (Q_2): 50th percentile = median;
  • (Q_3): 75th percentile.

Each interval between successive quartile boundaries contains roughly one quarter of the observations.

Interquartile range (IQR)

The interquartile range is:

[ IQR = Q_3 - Q_1. ]

It describes the width of the middle 50% of a distribution.

Because it ignores the most extreme quarter at each end, it is much less sensitive to outliers than the total range.

Median + IQR therefore form a natural descriptive pair.

x <- c(3, 5, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12)

quantile(x, probs = c(0.25, 0.50, 0.75))
25% 50% 75% 
  7   8  10 
IQR(x)
[1] 3

Gonick and Smith’s illustrated treatment makes the same geometry visible: the box in a boxplot runs from (Q_1) to (Q_3), with the median drawn inside (Gonick and Smith 2019).

Boxplots: a compact display of center and spread

A conventional Tukey boxplot shows:

  • lower edge of box = (Q_1);
  • line inside box = median;
  • upper edge of box = (Q_3);
  • box width in the measurement dimension = IQR;
  • whiskers extending to observations within the conventional 1.5-IQR limits;
  • observations beyond the whiskers plotted individually.
ImportantA boxplot ‘outlier’ is not automatically an error

The 1.5-IQR rule identifies observations that are unusual relative to the distribution.

It does not tell you why they are unusual.

An unusual archaeological value may represent:

  • a recording error;
  • a laboratory problem;
  • a different age or biological condition;
  • a real minority behavior;
  • chronological mixing;
  • a distinct social group; or
  • an entirely legitimate extreme case.

Inspect the observation and its context before deciding what it means.

Variance and standard deviation

Why simple deviations cannot measure total spread

The deviation of an observation from the sample mean is:

[ d_i = x_i-{x}. ]

But positive and negative deviations cancel:

[ d_i = 0. ]

So we square them.

Sample variance

For a sample:

[ s^2 = . ]

Variance is the average squared distance around the mean, with the sample correction (n-1).

The numerator,

[ (x_i-{x})^2, ]

is called the sum of squares.

Perkins walks through this logic by subtracting the mean from each value, squaring the deviations, summing them, dividing by (n-1), and then taking the square root to obtain SD (Perkins 2026).

Why (n-1)?

If the data are treated as a sample used to estimate population variability, dividing by (n) tends to underestimate the population variance.

Using (n-1) is the familiar Bessel correction.

R’s:

var(x)
sd(x)

use the sample formulas with (n-1).

For Assignment 1, that is exactly what students should use.

Standard deviation

The standard deviation is:

[ s=. ]

Variance has squared units:

  • mm²;
  • g²;
  • ‰².

Taking the square root returns the result to the original measurement units.

That is why SD is generally easier to interpret than variance.

Dressler emphasizes this return to the original metric: the squaring needed to construct variance makes the units difficult to interpret, while the square root restores them (Dressler 2015).

NoteInterpret SD as spread, not as a guaranteed boundary

A larger SD means observations are, on average, more dispersed around the mean.

It does not mean every observation falls within one SD of the mean.

The empirical rule

For a distribution that is approximately normal:

  • about 68% of observations lie within ({x}1s);
  • about 95% lie within ({x}2s).

The Week 4 lecture gives the more precise values of approximately 68.26% and 95.44%.

WarningThe empirical rule is conditional

The 68–95 pattern is a property of an approximately normal, symmetric, mound-shaped distribution.

Do not mechanically apply it to:

  • strongly skewed data;
  • heavily multimodal data;
  • distributions with influential extremes; or
  • variables bounded in ways that make the normal model implausible.

Also, ({x}1s) and ({x}2s) describe the spread of observations under a normal model. They are not confidence intervals for the population mean.

Robust and relative measures of spread

Median absolute deviation (MAD)

A robust alternative to SD is the median absolute deviation:

[ MAD = (|x_i-(x)|). ]

Like the median and IQR, MAD is resistant to extreme observations.

In R, note an important detail:

x <- c(3, 5, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 80)

# Raw median absolute deviation from the median:
mad(x, constant = 1)
[1] 2
# R's default is scaled to estimate SD under normality:
mad(x)
[1] 2.9652

MAD is not required for Assignment 1, but it provides a useful comparison when distributions are strongly skewed or contain influential extremes.

Coefficient of variation (CV)

For an appropriate ratio-scale variable:

[ CV=%. ]

CV expresses variability relative to the magnitude of the mean.

This matters when absolute SD naturally increases with body size or scale.

Bioanthropological application

Plavcan shows why relative variability matters in studies of living primates and fossil hominins. An SD measured in grams or millimeters will naturally be larger for a large-bodied species than for a small-bodied species, even if their relative variability is similar (Plavcan 2012).

The CV places variation on a relative scale.

Plavcan’s comparative data also show why simple universal cutoffs are dangerous: within-sex variation differs by trait. Many craniofacial dimensions in living primates have CVs below 10%, while body-mass CVs can be around 12–15%; fossil interpretation must also consider geography, time, sex, taxonomy, sample quality, and trait choice (Plavcan 2012).

WarningCV is not a universal standardization detector

The lecture uses CV to illustrate technological standardization and biological size variation.

A low CV can be consistent with standardized production, but it does not by itself prove specialist production.

Sampling, raw material, function, measurement protocol, temporal mixing, and the scale of the mean can all affect observed variability.

Why Assignment 1 does not use CV

Assignment 1 compares (^{13}) and (^{15}) delta values.

Because their zero points are reference points rather than true absence, a ratio such as (s/{x}) does not have the interpretation required for CV.

Use the assignment’s requested measures:

  • SD;
  • IQR;
  • minimum;
  • maximum; and
  • range.

Distribution shape changes the meaning of the center

Symmetric distributions

In a reasonably symmetric unimodal distribution:

[ . ]

Mean + SD can often provide an effective compact description.

Positive / right skew

A right-skewed distribution has a long tail toward larger values.

Extreme high values pull the mean upward:

[ > ]

is a common pattern.

Negative / left skew

A left-skewed distribution has a long tail toward smaller values.

Extreme low values pull the mean downward:

[ < . ]

Skewness is a clue, not a verdict

Mean–median differences can signal asymmetry, but they are not a complete diagnostic.

Always inspect the distribution itself.

Perkins’s R chapter demonstrates why: a histogram reveals tail structure and unusual observations that a table of descriptive statistics can summarize but cannot fully display (Perkins 2026).

NotePair the statistic with the picture

A useful Week 4 habit is:

histogram → boxplot → numerical summaries → interpretation

not:

mean → conclusion.

Histograms: the shape depends partly on the bins

A histogram groups a continuous variable into intervals.

It helps reveal:

  • symmetry;
  • skewness;
  • tails;
  • gaps;
  • possible multimodality; and
  • unusual values.

But the apparent shape can change when the number or width of bins changes.

Pivitera emphasizes that grouped frequency displays are constructed representations of the underlying observations (Privitera 2024). Gonick and Smith make the same point visually by building histograms from grouped frequency intervals (Gonick and Smith 2019).

When you make a histogram in R, try several reasonable values of bins, such as:

geom_histogram(bins = 8)
geom_histogram(bins = 10)
geom_histogram(bins = 12)

If an apparent “peak” disappears immediately when the binning changes slightly, be cautious about interpreting it as real multimodality.

Anthropological and archaeological applications

Variation can itself be evidence

Dispersion is not merely a technical statistic added after the mean.

Variation can be the phenomenon of interest.

In biological anthropology, variation may bear on:

  • sexual dimorphism;
  • taxonomic heterogeneity;
  • geographic variation;
  • developmental plasticity; or
  • environmental effects.

Plavcan’s early-Homo analysis treats size variation as evidence that must be interpreted comparatively and cautiously rather than as noise to be removed (Plavcan 2012).

Archaeological standardization

Archaeologists can compare dispersion in:

  • ceramic dimensions;
  • projectile-point measurements;
  • brick sizes;
  • weight systems; or
  • other manufactured objects.

A tightly clustered distribution may be consistent with stronger standardization; a broader distribution may indicate greater heterogeneity.

But Banning’s larger methodological warning remains essential: apparent variation can also arise from measurement protocol, analyst decisions, sampling, classification, or the archaeological formation process (Banning 2020).

Thus:

variation becomes archaeological evidence only after we understand how the variation was produced.

Means can also be misleading because of measurement decisions

Banning gives an especially important archaeological example: average artifact size depends on what counts as a measurable artifact. If one analyst includes fragments down to 1 cm and another includes only pieces above 2 cm, they can obtain different means from the same underlying material (Banning 2020).

The lesson connects Week 3 to Week 4:

descriptive statistics inherit every decision built into measurement and sampling.

Assignment 1 case study — Bronze Age diet and farming

El Argar, La Bastida, and Gatas

Assignment 1 is based on Knipper et al.’s study of the El Argar society of southeast Iberia, approximately 2200–1550 cal BCE (Knipper et al. 2020).

El Argar was a complex Bronze Age society characterized by:

  • fortified settlements;
  • cereal agriculture;
  • metallurgy;
  • hierarchical social organization; and
  • marked funerary inequality.

The study compares La Bastida and Gatas, two hilltop settlements with social, economic, and environmental differences.

Knipper and colleagues analyzed several positions in the food web:

cereals → domestic animals → humans

They measured stable carbon and nitrogen isotope composition in charred cereal grains and in animal and human bone collagen (Knipper et al. 2020).

This broader design matters because the Assignment 1 teaching file contains only the human observations.

The usable human sample

The original study sampled 75 humans:

  • 52 from La Bastida;
  • 23 from Gatas.

Six samples were excluded from interpretation because collagen preservation was insufficient.

The evaluable human results are therefore:

  • La Bastida: 48
  • Gatas: 21
  • Total: 69

These 69 observations form the Assignment 1 teaching dataset (Knipper et al. 2020).

What δ13C and δ15N can tell us

Stable carbon and nitrogen isotope values are useful for reconstructing aspects of food webs and diet.

In broad terms:

  • (^{13}) reflects carbon sources and ecological inputs into the food web;
  • (^{15}) generally increases across trophic levels and is often related to dietary protein sources.

But Knipper et al. stress that environmental and agricultural processes also influence isotope baselines (Knipper et al. 2020).

For example:

  • plant water conditions can affect carbon-isotope values;
  • manuring can raise crop (^{15});
  • animal feeding practices affect faunal isotope baselines;
  • breastfeeding and metabolic stress can affect values in subadults.

Therefore, a human isotope difference is not automatically a direct dietary difference.

The crucial archaeological lesson

The human values at La Bastida are higher on average for both (^{13}) and (^{15}) than at Gatas in the original study (Knipper et al. 2020).

A tempting interpretation would be:

Higher human (^{15}) at La Bastida = more meat or dairy.

The full food-web evidence makes that inference unsafe.

Knipper et al. found differences in cereal and animal baselines as well. Their results indicate extensive rain-fed cereal cultivation with little to moderate manuring, and especially at La Bastida they found strong integration between crop production and domestic-animal feeding. When plant and animal baselines are considered, higher human isotope values do not necessarily imply different average dietary composition (Knipper et al. 2020).

Step 9 — Understand every column in the summary

n

How many non-missing observations contributed to that site × isotope summary?

mean

The arithmetic balance point.

Ask whether extreme values appear to pull it away from the main body of the distribution.

median

The 50th percentile.

Ask whether it differs meaningfully from the mean.

sd

The sample standard deviation.

It is expressed in the same unit as the isotope measurement: ‰.

iqr

The width of the middle 50%.

It is resistant to the tails and pairs naturally with the median.

min, max, and range

These describe the extremes and total observed span.

Because range uses only minimum and maximum, interpret it together with the histogram, boxplot, SD, and IQR.

Step 11 — Compare SD with IQR

SD and IQR do not measure exactly the same thing, so do not compare their raw magnitudes as if they should be equal.

Instead ask:

  • Does the boxplot show a compact middle 50% but long tails?
  • Does the range look large relative to the IQR?
  • Are one or two observations influencing the SD?
  • Are the data reasonably symmetric so that mean + SD give a useful compact description?
  • Would median + IQR more faithfully represent the center and central spread?

A good descriptive report can present both:

  • median + IQR

Common errors in Week 4

Error 1 — Calculating a mean because the column is numeric

Ask what the numbers mean.

Codes for categories are not quantitative distances.

Error 2 — Reporting only the mean

Always consider spread and shape.

Error 3 — Calling every extreme observation an error

Investigate it.

An unusual value can be a mistake, but it can also be substantive evidence.

Error 4 — Removing outliers to make the graph look normal

Do not modify the data simply to obtain a preferred distribution.

Any exclusion requires a documented substantive or data-quality reason.

Error 5 — Treating SD as meaningful without the distribution

SD is most interpretable when considered with shape, sample size, and the mean.

Error 6 — Treating ±2 SD as a confidence interval for the mean

It is not.

The empirical rule concerns the expected spread of observations under an approximately normal distribution.

Error 7 — Using CV on interval-scale data

CV requires a meaningful ratio zero.

Do not calculate CV for Assignment 1’s delta isotope values.

Error 8 — Interpreting a site difference as a cause

A descriptive difference does not identify a mechanism.

Error 9 — Forgetting unequal sample sizes

La Bastida has 48 observations; Gatas has 21.

A smaller sample often produces a rougher-looking histogram and less stable extremes.

That does not make the smaller sample invalid, but it should temper visual interpretation.

Error 10 — Rounding too early

Round the displayed table, not the source data.

  • Raw data need organization. A table of measurements is evidence, but not yet a clear description.
  • Every distribution has a center and a spread. Report both.
  • Measurement scale comes first. A statistic is meaningful only when the variable supports the operation.
  • Mode finds peaks. It is essential for nominal categories and useful for recognizing multimodality.
  • Median is resistant. It is especially useful for ordinal, skewed, or outlier-prone data.
  • Mean is a balance point. It uses every observation and is therefore both powerful and sensitive.
  • Range is fragile. It depends only on the extremes.
  • IQR describes the middle 50%. It pairs naturally with the median.
  • Variance uses squared deviations. Sample variance uses (n-1).
  • SD restores the original units. It pairs naturally with the mean when distribution shape makes that useful.
  • MAD protects against extreme values. It is a robust spread measure.
  • CV measures relative spread. Use it only when a ratio scale and meaningful nonzero mean justify it.
  • Shape matters. Mean–median divergence, tails, gaps, and multimodality require visual inspection.
  • Outliers are questions, not garbage. Investigate them before deciding what they mean.
  • Archaeological variability can be substantive evidence. It can inform questions about standardization, dimorphism, taxonomy, heterogeneity, and social differentiation.
  • Assignment 1 is descriptive. It asks what the human isotope distributions show, not for an inferential test.
  • Context controls interpretation. Knipper et al.’s cereal and animal baselines show why a human isotope difference is not automatically a dietary mechanism.
  • Describe before explaining. This is the central Week 4 habit and the central logic of Assignment 1.
