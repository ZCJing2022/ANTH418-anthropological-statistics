Week 8 — Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

Estimate → quantify uncertainty → test against a null model → interpret archaeologically

Overview

Statistical inference asks us to move from an observed sample to a larger population while remaining explicit about uncertainty. In archaeology, that move is especially difficult because specimens have already passed through preservation, deposition, excavation, recovery, curation, identification, and analytical selection.

This week follows one connected sequence:

Estimate → quantify uncertainty → test against a null model → interpret archaeologically

It forms three conceptual movements: (I) estimate and quantify uncertainty; (II) test against a null model; and (III) interpret archaeologically. Statistical inference is one component of archaeological inference, not a substitute for it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish populations, samples, parameters, statistics, and estimates;
  • explain sampling variability and why estimates require measures of uncertainty;
  • distinguish point estimates from interval estimates;
  • interpret a 95% confidence interval appropriately;
  • explain bootstrap confidence intervals;
  • distinguish bootstrap distributions from permutation null distributions;
  • state null and alternative hypotheses;
  • explain a p-value as a tail probability under a specified null model;
  • distinguish statistical significance, effect magnitude, uncertainty, and archaeological significance;
  • identify limits created by sampling, non-independence, exchangeability, measurement, chronology, and comparison design; and
  • trace the inferential chain in the Teotihuacan leporid case.

Required Readings

  • Cumming, G., & Calin-Jageman, R. (2024). Introduction to the new statistics: Estimation, open science, and beyond (2nd ed.). Routledge. Chapters 5–6.
  • Somerville, A. D., Sugiyama, N., Manzanilla, L. R., & Schoeninger, M. J. (2016). Animal management at the ancient metropolis of Teotihuacan, Mexico: Stable isotope analysis of leporid (cottontail and jackrabbit) bone mineral. PLOS ONE, 11(8), e0159982.
  • Thulin, M. (2024). Modern statistics with R: From wrangling and exploring data to inference and predictive modelling (2nd ed.). Chapman & Hall/CRC. Chapter 3 sections on hypothesis testing, p-values, and confidence intervals.

Movement I — Estimate → Quantify Uncertainty

1. From Samples to Populations

A population is the larger set of cases about which we want to learn. A sample is the set we observe. A parameter describes a population; a statistic is calculated from a sample.

Population quantity Sample estimate
Mean, \(\mu\) Mean, \(\bar{x}\)
Variance, \(\sigma^2\) Variance, \(s^2\)
Proportion, \(P\) Proportion, \(\hat{p}\)
Difference, \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) Difference, \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\)

A parameter is treated as fixed but usually unknown. Sample statistics vary from sample to sample. This sampling variability is why estimates need an account of uncertainty (Cumming & Calin-Jageman, 2024).

Archaeological complication

Archaeological samples are rarely simple random samples. Before inference, ask:

  1. What is the target population?
  2. What exactly is the observed sample?
  3. How did preservation, excavation, recovery, curation, and analytical selection produce it?
  4. Which cases could not enter it?
  5. What assumptions permit the sample to inform the population?

Key idea: More sophisticated statistics do not automatically make an archaeological sample representative.

2. Point Estimates versus Interval Estimates

A point estimate is a single numerical estimate. It answers “How much?” An interval estimate adds information about precision.

For a mean, a conventional confidence interval has the general form

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{\mathrm{crit}}\times SE, \qquad SE=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

The standard error describes how much an estimate such as the sample mean would vary across repeated samples. It is not the standard deviation of the individual observations.

Larger samples generally produce narrower intervals; greater variability generally produces wider intervals. But precision is not correctness: a narrow interval around a biased estimate can be precisely wrong.

3. What Does a 95% Confidence Interval Mean?

In the standard frequentist interpretation, if the same sampling-and-interval procedure were repeated many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the fixed population parameter, assuming the method and assumptions are appropriate (Cumming & Calin-Jageman, 2024).

Do not say that a particular frequentist interval gives a 95% probability that the fixed parameter lies inside it.

Instead ask: Where is the estimate? How wide is the interval? Which effect magnitudes remain compatible with the data and assumptions? Are those magnitudes archaeologically meaningful?

Do not turn a confidence interval into another binary significance detector by looking only at whether it crosses zero.

4. Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

The bootstrap approximates uncertainty by resampling:

  1. start with the observed sample of size \(n\);
  2. draw \(n\) observations with replacement;
  3. calculate the statistic;
  4. store the estimate;
  5. repeat many times;
  6. examine the bootstrap distribution; and
  7. use that distribution to quantify uncertainty.

A simple percentile 95% interval uses approximately the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap estimates.

Bootstrap: How much might my estimate vary?

The bootstrap distribution is a distribution of repeatedly calculated statistics, not individual specimens.

Bootstrapping cannot repair biased preservation, recovery, context selection, laboratory selection, a mismatch between sample and target population, or non-independence. Ten thousand resamples still contain only the information supplied by the original data.

Movement II — Test Against a Null Model

5. Estimation and Testing Ask Different Questions

Estimation asks: How large is the effect, and how uncertain is the estimate?

Testing asks: How unusual would the observed result be under a specified null model?

A useful analysis often needs both. Estimate first; then ask whether the observed pattern is unusual under a clearly defined reference model.

6. Null and Alternative Hypotheses

For two population means:

\[ H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0 \]

\[ H_A:\mu_1-\mu_2\neq0 \]

The null hypothesis is a statistical reference model. It is not necessarily an archaeological claim that “nothing happened.”

Likewise, “the population isotope means differ” is not equivalent to “people managed leporids.” The first is statistical; the second proposes human behavior. Moving between them requires archaeological theory, context, chronology, ecology, and alternatives.

7. Permutation Tests and Null Distributions

A permutation test constructs a null distribution by rearranging data in a way that represents the null hypothesis:

  1. calculate the observed statistic;
  2. keep measured values fixed;
  3. shuffle group labels as permitted by the null model;
  4. preserve group sizes;
  5. recalculate the statistic;
  6. repeat many times; and
  7. locate the observed statistic in the resulting null distribution.

Permutation: What would differences look like if the null model held?

Its validity depends on exchangeability: which observations can legitimately have their labels shuffled?

Bootstrap Permutation
Resample observations with replacement Usually shuffle labels
Quantifies uncertainty in an estimate Evaluates compatibility with a null model
Produces a bootstrap distribution Produces a null distribution
Commonly supports confidence intervals Commonly supports p-values

Both are resampling methods, but they answer different questions (Thulin, 2024).

8. p-Values as Tail Probabilities

A p-value is calculated under a specified null model:

\[ p=P(\text{result as extreme or more extreme}\mid H_0\text{ and assumptions}). \]

A small p-value places the observed statistic relatively far into the tails of the null distribution. A large p-value means that the result is not unusual under that model; it does not prove the null is true.

A p-value is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true, the probability that \(H_A\) is true, the probability that chance caused the result, the probability that this result is a false positive, the effect size, or the probability that an archaeological explanation is correct (Cumming & Calin-Jageman, 2024; Hubbard, 2011).

9. Beyond the .05 Bright Line

Students need to understand the conventional p < .05 rule because it remains common. But it should not become a decision ritual. Wasserstein et al. (2019) argue against drawing conclusions solely from whether a p-value crosses an arbitrary threshold. Values such as p = .049 and p = .051 should not be treated as fundamentally different scientific outcomes.

A fuller interpretation combines:

Magnitude + uncertainty + context

Movement III — Interpret Archaeologically

10. Statistical Significance versus Archaeological Significance

Statistical significance concerns data relative to a statistical model and decision convention. Archaeological significance concerns what a pattern means for understanding past practices, institutions, environments, chronology, or other processes.

The two can diverge. A tiny effect can become statistically detectable in a large sample; an archaeologically important effect can remain uncertain in a small sample; and a strong-looking pattern can reflect biased sampling, chronological mixing, or non-independent observations.

The archaeological question is therefore not merely:

Is p < .05?

It is:

How large is the pattern, how uncertain is it, what archaeological process could generate it, and what other evidence makes that process more or less plausible?

Case Study — Teotihuacan Leporid Management

11. The Archaeological Problem

Somerville et al. (2016) asked whether cottontails and jackrabbits at Teotihuacan were simply hunted or whether some were also provisioned, managed, or bred.

The case contains the full inferential chain:

archaeological question → measurement → comparison → uncertainty/testing → archaeological interpretation

The authors analyzed stable carbon and oxygen isotopes from leporid bone mineral. Greater consumption of C4/CAM plants—including maize and other plants associated with human cultivation—can elevate leporid \(\delta^{13}C\) values. But an isotope measurement is not a direct observation of someone feeding an animal.

12. Why Oztoyahualco Matters

Oztoyahualco 15B was archaeologically relevant before the isotope test. The compound contained an unusually large leporid assemblage and contextual evidence associated with animal processing and possible keeping (Somerville et al., 2016).

This motivates a prediction:

If leporids at Oztoyahualco were unusually integrated into human provisioning, their carbon isotope values should differ in the predicted direction from appropriate comparison contexts.

The statistical analysis therefore follows an archaeological question rather than searching for any contrast that happens to yield a small p-value.

13. Begin with the Estimate

Somerville et al. (2016) reported a mean \(\delta^{13}C_{apatite}\) of approximately −5.9‰ for Oztoyahualco leporids and approximately −8.3‰ for the combined other archaeological contexts: an observed contrast of about +2.4‰. Their dietary interpretation associated the Oztoyahualco mean with approximately 74% C4/CAM plant consumption.

Before asking whether the difference is “significant,” ask:

  • What is its direction and magnitude?
  • How variable are observations within groups?
  • How uncertain is the estimated contrast?
  • Is +2.4‰ meaningful in isotope-ecological terms?
  • Are the groups archaeologically and chronologically appropriate comparisons?

14. Test Against the Null

Somerville et al. (2016) reported that the Oztoyahualco \(\delta^{13}C_{apatite}\) mean was higher than the combined mean of the other archaeological locations (p = .001).

This does not mean there is a .1% probability that leporid management did not occur. The test addresses a statistical contrast relative to a null model; it does not assign a probability to the archaeological explanation.

15. Build the Archaeological Interpretation

The interpretive chain is approximately:

\[ \text{higher }\delta^{13}C \rightarrow \text{greater C4/CAM consumption} \rightarrow \text{access to cultivated resources} \rightarrow \text{possible provisioning} \rightarrow \text{possible management/breeding}. \]

Every arrow requires evidence.

Somerville et al. (2016) strengthen the interpretation by combining isotopic results with archaeological context. Alternatives remain possible, including crop raiding, garden hunting, movement of animals, and mixtures of managed and wild animals.

The supplementary collagen study (Somerville et al., 2017) shows why binary significance is inadequate. Variation among animals, multiple leporid taxa, and isotope variance inconsistent with highly standardized production complicate a simple closed-system breeding model.

Limits of Inference

16. Sampling

A confidence interval can be numerically precise while representing the wrong population. Preservation, excavation strategy, recovery, curation, laboratory selection, and chronological aggregation affect what a sample can represent.

Ask: What population can these observations legitimately represent?

17. Independence and Exchangeability

Archaeological observations are often clustered: several bones may derive from one animal, artifacts from one feature, features from one household, and households from one neighborhood. Treating correlated observations as independent inflates the apparent amount of information and may make uncertainty too small.

For permutation tests:

Ask: What exactly may legitimately be shuffled?

18. What a Statistical Test Cannot Establish

A statistical test cannot by itself establish causation, mechanism, representativeness, correct chronology, correct measurement, or archaeological meaning. A small p-value cannot choose among competing archaeological explanations unless the research design and contextual evidence make those alternatives distinguishable.

R Workflow for Week 8

# 1. Inspect the data
glimpse(dat)

# 2. Estimate the observed group difference
dat |>
  group_by(group) |>
  summarize(
    n = n(),
    mean = mean(value, na.rm = TRUE),
    sd = sd(value, na.rm = TRUE)
  )

# 3. Bootstrap uncertainty
# resample observations with replacement
# calculate the difference each time
# inspect the bootstrap distribution and interval

# 4. Permutation null model
# shuffle group labels as justified by the design
# calculate the difference each time
# build the null distribution

# 5. Locate the observed statistic in the null distribution

# 6. Interpret magnitude + uncertainty + archaeological context

Do not memorize code without understanding what is being resampled or shuffled. The computational operation must correspond to the inferential question.

Key Distinctions to Remember

Do not confuse Correct distinction
Sample statistic / population parameter Observed estimate / target quantity
SD / SE Variation among observations / uncertainty of an estimate
Point estimate / CI Estimated magnitude / estimated precision
Bootstrap / permutation Estimate uncertainty / null-model comparison
p-value / \(\alpha\) Data-dependent tail probability / pre-specified threshold
p-value / effect size Null-model compatibility / magnitude
Statistical / archaeological significance Statistical convention / substantive interpretation
Statistical alternative / archaeological explanation Population pattern / explanation of past behavior

Study Questions

  1. Why is the distinction between a sample and a population especially complicated in archaeology?
  2. Why is a point estimate incomplete without an account of uncertainty?
  3. What does a 95% confidence interval mean in frequentist statistics?
  4. Why should a confidence interval not be reduced to whether it crosses zero?
  5. What exactly is resampled in a bootstrap?
  6. What is shuffled in a permutation test, and why does exchangeability matter?
  7. How do bootstrap and permutation distributions answer different questions?
  8. What does a p-value tell you? Name at least four things it does not tell you.
  9. Why should p = .049 and p = .051 not lead to radically different scientific interpretations?
  10. Why does the Teotihuacan isotope contrast not by itself demonstrate leporid management?
  11. What independent archaeological evidence makes the Oztoyahualco interpretation more plausible?
  12. Which alternative explanations remain possible?
  13. How could non-independence make an apparently strong statistical result misleading?
  14. Explain in your own words: estimate → uncertainty → null model → archaeological interpretation.

Take-Home Message

Start with the effect, not the threshold.

Estimate the pattern. Quantify how uncertain the estimate is. If a null comparison is useful, define the null model and understand what the test simulates. Then return to the archaeological question.

A p-value can tell us how unusual an observed result is under a specified null model. It cannot tell us what happened in the past. That conclusion requires statistical evidence to be combined with archaeological context, measurement knowledge, chronology, theory, and competing explanations.
